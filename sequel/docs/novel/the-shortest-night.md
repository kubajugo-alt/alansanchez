# Alan Sanches II: The Shortest Night

## Prologue: The Longest Night

*Gran Canaria. The twenty-first of December, 1995. The winter solstice.*

The bar in Artenara had been closing for an hour.

It was the kind of mountain bar that never quite shut. The shutters came down halfway, the television went off, and Paco began putting the chairs up on the tables, one at a time, with the patience of a man who knew that the last customer would leave when she was ready and not before.

The last customer was standing at the telephone on the wall, feeding it coins.

Dr. Elena Suárez was forty-five years old, and she had been awake since five that morning. Her field jacket was the colour of dust, because it was mostly dust. Her hair was tied up in a knot with a pencil pushed through it, which was how she always wore it, and which Carmen said made her look like a woman who had been interrupted in the middle of a thought. Elena said that was accurate. She was always in the middle of a thought.

Tonight the thought had teeth.

The phone rang four times in a flat in Las Palmas, far below, and then the answering machine picked up. Carmen's voice, recorded, smoky and bored.

"Trujillo. Speak, or don't."

The beep.

"Carmen. It's me." Elena turned her back to the room and lowered her voice, though Paco was the only one there, and Paco had heard everything in forty years and repeated none of it. "I know it's late. Listen."

She stopped. She hadn't decided how to say it. She had driven the dirt track down from the caves with the headlights off for the first kilometre, because her hands had been shaking and she hadn't wanted anyone at the camp to see the car leave.

"Somebody's taken one of the discs out of the channel," she said. "In Cave 6. The wear marks are fresh. Steel, Carmen. Someone used a chisel. It isn't conservation. Nobody told me about any conservation."

The coins dropped inside the phone. She fed it another.

"And it isn't a calendar either. That's the other thing. I've been reading it wrong for a year. A whole year. I kept thinking the light was counting something." She laughed, once, without any pleasure in it. "It's not counting. It's a door, Carmen. The whole cave. It's a door, and the discs keep it shut. And someone's opening it."

Behind her, a chair scraped on the tiles.

"I'm driving down now," she said. "Don't smoke all of them before I get there."

She was quiet for a moment. Then, more gently:

"Te quiero."

She hung up.

Paco did not look up from the chairs. "There's cloud on the road, doctora," he said. "Thick tonight. Stay. My wife will make up the room."

"There's always cloud on the road," said Elena, and she smiled at him, and went out into the cold.

---

The Renault 4 was older than some of her students. It started on the second try, which for December was a good omen.

She sat for a moment with the engine running and the heater roaring uselessly, and looked back up the mountain. Above her, on the ridge, the lights of the dig camp were still on. As she watched, they went out, one by one, until only a single window was lit.

"Rayco's still up," she said aloud, fondly. "He never sleeps."

Then she put the car in gear and began the long way down.

The road from Artenara falls off the top of Gran Canaria like a dropped ribbon. It loops and doubles back on itself, hairpin after hairpin, with pine trees on one side and nothing at all on the other. In daylight it is one of the most beautiful roads in the world. At night, in winter, it is a series of short white tunnels made by your own headlights, and beyond them, nothing.

And below, always, the cloud.

On most nights of the year a sea of cloud lies over the north of the island, flat and solid, a thousand metres down. From above, by moonlight, it looks like a frozen ocean, silver and still, as if you could step off the edge of the road and walk across it to Tenerife. Elena had lived under that cloud all her life and above it for most of her working years, and she had never stopped finding it beautiful.

Tonight it was lit silver by the moon, and it was coming up to meet her.

She turned the radio on for company. Radio Las Palmas, the late folk programme: a timple, the little Canarian guitar, quick and bright, and a woman singing an old song about a fisherman's wife. Elena hummed along with it, badly.

"Still awake," she said to the radio. "Someone has to be."

On the passenger seat her notebook lay open, where she had thrown it. Even in the dashboard glow she could see the sketch she had made that afternoon: the wall of Cave 6, the line of the light channel, and seven small circles drawn along it. Six she had filled in solid. The seventh was an empty outline, gone over again and again with angry strokes of the pencil, so hard that the paper had torn.

"Six," she said quietly. "There should be seven."

The air in the car was stale and too warm. She reached across and wound the window down an inch, and the cold came in, sharp and clean, smelling of pine resin and stone.

"Smells like mountains," she said. "Good."

Then the car went into the cloud, and the world went grey.

---

It happened all at once, the way it always did. One moment there was moonlight and pine trees, and the next there was nothing but grey, pressing against the windscreen like wet wool. The headlights lit a wall a few metres ahead and showed her nothing beyond it. She slowed to a crawl, and leaned forward over the wheel, and drove by memory.

She had done it a hundred times.

The grey grew darker.

At first she thought it was only the cloud thickening, the way it did in the deeper folds of the barranco. But it went on darkening, from grey to charcoal, from charcoal to black, until the headlights were two weak yellow smudges and then not even that. It was like driving into ink.

On the radio the timple faltered. The woman's voice stretched, and thinned, and broke into static.

And under the static there was another sound.

Elena frowned and turned the volume up. It was a long, slow hiss, rising and falling, rising and falling, with a rhythm she knew as well as her own heartbeat, because she had grown up a street away from Las Canteras beach.

It was surf. Waves on sand, breaking and drawing back.

On a mountain road, a thousand metres above the sea.

She turned the radio off. The sound did not stop.

She reached for the window crank to wind the window back up, because the air coming in had changed. It wasn't cold any more. It was damp and heavy, and it smelled of something that did not belong here at all.

"Salt," she said. "Why is it salt?"

The crank would not turn. She pulled at it, then put her whole weight on it, and it would not move, as if the window had been set in stone.

"Come on," she said. "Come on."

She looked down.

There was water in the footwell.

It was black, and it was cold, and it was rising around her boots. Not dripping in from anywhere. Not running down from the dashboard. Rising, the way a tide rises, patiently, from below, with nowhere it could possibly be coming from.

She pressed the brake. The car did not slow. It went on rolling down the hairpins at the same gentle crawl, turning when the road turned, as if someone else were driving it, as if it knew the way.

She tried the door. It would not open. She tried the other door, leaning across, the water now at her knees, and it would not open either. She hammered on the glass with the side of her fist and the glass did not even ring. It was like hitting the side of a mountain.

The water reached her waist. It was so cold it burned.

Elena Suárez had spent her whole adult life reading the marks that other people left on stone. She was a patient woman, and a precise one, and when she understood that there was nothing left that she could do, she stopped doing things.

She stopped hammering on the glass. She stopped pulling at the door. She sat back in the driver's seat, and looked at the windscreen, which had fogged over completely with the cold and the salt.

She thought of Carmen, getting home late to the flat, seeing the little red light blinking on the answering machine, pressing play.

Then she raised one finger and drew on the glass.

Three lines. A triangle, with its point facing down. The same shape that was carved a hundred times on the walls of Cave 6, the shape the textbooks called a fertility symbol and that she had spent a year trying to understand.

The water reached her chest.

She kept her finger on the glass, at the point of the triangle, pointing down. Pointing at the sea.

Then there was only the dark, and the sound of the surf.

---

In the morning the sun came up over the island the way it does in winter, low and bright and clean, and the sea of cloud below the mountains burned off by ten o'clock.

A goatherd found the car.

It was sitting at the bottom of a dry barranco a little below the road, a narrow gully of red rock and grey spurge where no water had run for weeks. It was upright, and undamaged. There was no sign it had left the road at speed. There was no sign it had left the road at all. It was simply there, at the bottom of the gully, as if it had been set down gently by a very large hand.

The windows were closed. The doors were locked from the inside.

By noon there were two officers of the Guardia Civil standing beside it in the bright sun, sweating in their uniforms. One of them was taking photographs. He spent a long time on the windscreen, where a shape had dried into the glass in a fine white crust. A triangle, pointing down.

The crust was salt.

"Flash flood," said the older officer. "Has to be."

The younger one looked up and down the barranco. Red rock. Dust. A lizard on a stone, unbothered.

"There's no water in this barranco," he said.

The older officer lit a cigarette and looked at the car for a long time.

"Then it went somewhere," he said.

The Guardia Civil recorded the death of Dr. Elena Suárez as an accident caused by a flash flood. The report noted that her lungs were full of water, and that the water was salt.

It had not rained on that side of the mountain for forty days.

After that, for six months, nothing happened at all.

The Sea is patient. It answers only at a turning of the year.

And the next turning was June.

## Chapter One: Arrival

*Tuesday, the eighteenth of June, 1996.*

Alan Sanches had been on Gran Canaria for fifty-five minutes, and in that time he had lost his luggage, his dignity, and the skin on the back of his neck.

The luggage was in Madrid. The woman at the Iberia desk at Gando airport had told him so with enormous sympathy and no urgency at all, as if Madrid were a place suitcases went to retire. It would come tomorrow, she said. Or Thursday. It was June, she said, as though that explained it, and perhaps on Gran Canaria it did.

The dignity had gone shortly afterwards, in the taxi queue, when he had bent down to tie his shoelace and split the seam of his only trousers.

The neck had gone in the twenty minutes he'd spent standing on the kerb outside arrivals, waiting for a man who was late.

He was thirty-eight years old. He owned a bookshop in Lisbon, half of a cat, and one cream linen suit, which was currently somewhere in Madrid, folded around a bottle of good port he had bought as a present. He was wearing a T-shirt that said nothing at all, and trousers that now said a great deal, and he was beginning to feel the sun on the back of his neck the way you feel a stranger's eyes.

Then a white Land Rover with the Museo Canario's crest on the door came round the corner too fast, and stopped, and Vasco Teles got out of it, and Alan forgot about all of it at once.

Vasco had been on the island for a week. A week was apparently all it took. He was brown already, the deep, even brown of a man who had been outdoors since dawn every day, and there was dust in the grey at his temples and on his boots and on the knees of his trousers. He was wearing a shirt with the sleeves rolled up and no tie, which for Vasco was the equivalent of other men dancing on tables. He looked like he had slept four hours a night for a week and had never been happier in his life.

He was radiant. There was no other word. He was lit up from inside like a church on a feast day.

"You're here," said Vasco, and kissed him, quickly and firmly, on the mouth, in front of a taxi driver, two nuns and a family from Düsseldorf.

"I'm here," said Alan. "My trousers are not entirely here. My suitcase is in Madrid."

"Madrid," said Vasco, in the tone of a man who had heard of it but never been convinced it existed. He looked Alan up and down. "You're burnt."

"I've been standing here for twenty minutes."

"I know. I'm sorry. We found something at the back of Cave 6 this morning, a second channel, very small, I think it's for the equinox, and I couldn't leave until we'd drawn it." He was already taking Alan's carry-on bag, already turning back towards the Land Rover, already talking. "You'll see it. Not tomorrow. Thursday, perhaps. Rayco wants everything recorded before anyone else goes in. Alan, the light. Wait until you see what the light does in there. It comes in through the roof like a blade and it walks across the wall all afternoon, and every engraving it touches, it's like someone's reading a sentence out loud."

"Rayco," said Alan.

"Dr. Baute. The director. You'll meet him tonight."

"You call him Rayco."

Vasco paused with his hand on the door of the Land Rover. "He asked me to."

"Of course he did," said Alan, and climbed in.

---

They had been together for eight months.

Alan sometimes said the number to himself in the shop, under his breath, as he shelved books, the way you might press on a bruise to see if it still hurt. Eight months. Before Vasco, his longest relationship had been with a Swedish architect who had lasted eleven weeks and left behind a toothbrush and a strong opinion about Alan's kitchen. Before that there had been a lot of men, and none of them had stayed, and that had been the arrangement Alan liked best. Nobody stayed. Nobody noticed when you were late.

Vasco noticed when he was late.

Vasco had moved into the flat above the shop in January, the week he left the Patriarchate, with two suitcases of books and a coffee pot from his grandmother. He got up at six. He made the coffee strong and left a cup by Alan's side of the bed, and some mornings Alan woke up to the smell of it and the sound of Vasco reading the newspaper out loud to the cat, and felt something so close to happiness that it frightened him into the bathroom, where he examined his hair for grey and found it.

They had made rules, early on, because Vasco was a man who liked rules and Alan was a man who needed them. They weren't exclusive. Alan had been honest about that from the beginning, and Vasco, to his surprise, had simply nodded and said, all right, and then added two conditions, quietly, as if they were small.

Tell me first. Not after.

And never to settle a fight.

Alan had agreed at once. They had seemed like small conditions. In eight months he hadn't needed them. He had found, rather to his alarm, that he didn't want anyone else. He wanted the coffee by the bed.

That was the problem.

---

The road from the airport ran north along the coast, past cement works and banana plantations under grey netting and long white stretches of nothing, with the sea on the right, flat and pewter-coloured, and the island rising on the left, brown and steep and creased like a crumpled paper bag.

And over all of it, the cloud.

It had been blue sky at the airport. Halfway to Las Palmas they drove under the edge of it, and the light changed as if someone had closed a lid. It was a low, flat, unbroken ceiling of grey, so even and so close that it seemed to Alan you could reach up out of the window and touch it.

"Is it going to rain?" he asked.

Vasco laughed. "It never rains. It's the panza de burro."

"The what?"

"The donkey's belly. Every June the trade winds pile the cloud up against the north side of the island, and it just sits there. For weeks. Down in the city it's like this, grey and warm and close, like living in a steam room. And up in the mountains, above it, it's blazing sun." He was smiling at the windscreen. "Every morning I drive up through it. Ten minutes of fog, and then you come out on top and the whole cloud is underneath you like a sea. Like a white sea, all the way to the horizon. And Teide sticking out of it, on Tenerife, like an island."

"It sounds beautiful."

"It is." Vasco glanced at him. "You'll see it."

"Thursday, perhaps," said Alan.

Vasco heard it. He was quiet for a moment. Then he reached over and put his hand on Alan's knee, on the split seam, and left it there for the rest of the drive.

---

The hotel was on Las Canteras, a long curve of golden beach on the far side of Las Palmas, with a stone promenade running the length of it and a reef a few hundred metres out that broke the Atlantic into lazy, harmless waves. The hotel was not a good hotel. It had a lobby with rattan chairs and potted palms and a faded poster for the Fiestas de San Juan, and a lift that announced each floor with a sound like a sneeze. The expedition had booked it because it was cheap and the owner sponsored the dig.

Behind the reception desk stood a woman of about thirty-five in a crisp navy uniform, with her hair pulled back in a bun so tight it looked structural. Her name badge said NIEVES.

"Señor Sanches," she said, before he had opened his mouth. Her English was perfect. "Welcome. Your luggage has not arrived."

"No."

"And you have," she said, and her eyes travelled down to his trousers, and paused there, and came back up. One eyebrow rose about two millimetres. It was the most eloquent eyebrow Alan had ever seen.

"There's a reception tonight," said Vasco. "At the museum. Jacket required."

Nieves considered this. Then she bent down behind the desk and came up with a cardboard box, which she placed on the counter between them with a certain ceremony. On the side, in black marker, someone had written OBJETOS PERDIDOS.

"Lost and found," she said. "The Germans leave a great many jackets. They arrive in jackets, and then they discover the weather."

There were four jackets in the box. One was a ski anorak. One was a blazer with gold buttons and a yacht club crest. One was a child's. The last was a linen jacket in a colour that might once have been navy, and might once have been on a smaller man, and smelled very faintly of sun cream and someone else's cigarettes.

Alan put it on. The sleeves stopped a good two inches above his wrists.

Nieves looked at him for a long moment.

"Very elegant," she said, in a voice like a closing door.

---

There was an hour before the reception. Vasco went up to shower, and Alan went out to find a telephone.

There was a phone box on the corner by the promenade, and next to it a kiosk, a little green hut hung with newspapers pegged on a line like washing. Behind the counter sat a round man in his fifties with a magnificent moustache and a transistor radio held to one ear, listening to the football.

"I need change," said Alan, in Spanish. "For the telephone. To call Lisbon."

The man looked at him over the radio. He looked at the jacket.

"Lisbon," he said thoughtfully. "That's a lot of coins."

"I know."

"You're with the doctor. The Portuguese doctor, at the dig. The one who smiles."

Alan blinked. "How do you know that?"

"I'm Chano," said the man, as if that answered it, and perhaps it did. He counted out a stack of hundred-peseta coins and slid them across the counter. "You buy a newspaper, I give you a good rate. You don't buy a newspaper, I give you the tourist rate."

Alan bought a newspaper.

"Good," said Chano. "Now I tell you a secret, for free." He leaned forward. "Don't swim after dark this week."

"Why not?"

Chano shrugged and went back to his football. "The sea's in a bad mood," he said. "My knees can tell."

---

The phone rang twice in a bookshop in Lisbon, in the Rua dos Remédios, and then Inês Carvalho picked up and said, without preamble:

"Pessoa has been sick on the Eça de Queirós."

"Hello, Inês."

"On the first edition. The one in the window."

"Is he all right?"

"He's fine. He's asleep on the Saramago. The Eça is not fine." A pause. "How is it? How's the sun? How's the boyfriend? How is the man who leaves the shop to me for a week, three weeks before I submit my thesis, which I am not writing, because I'm minding his shop and his cat?"

"You could close the shop."

"I could, but then I would have to write my thesis." He could hear her lighting a cigarette. "Well?"

Alan looked out of the phone box. Across the promenade the beach was emptying in the evening. Families were folding umbrellas. A group of young surfers, local boys, were coming in out of the water with their boards under their arms, their wetsuits peeled down to the waist, their hair bleached into stiff gold ropes by the salt, laughing about something. One of them shook his head like a dog and the water flew off him in a bright spray.

Alan watched them go past.

"When did surfers get so young?" he said.

"What?"

"Oh," said Alan. "When I didn't."

"Alan."

"It's fine," he said. "It's lovely. He's lovely. He's happy, Inês. He's so happy. He's been here a week and he's already a different person. He talks about the cave like it's a person. He calls his boss by his first name."

There was a pause on the line.

"And you're jealous," said Inês.

"Of a cave."

"Of a cave," she agreed. "Alan. Go to the party. Be charming. Don't break anything."

"I'm wearing a German's jacket."

"I don't want to know," said Inês, and the money ran out.

---

El Museo Canario was in Vegueta, the old town, in a long building of pale stone and dark wooden balconies around a courtyard, two streets back from the cathedral. It was the oldest part of Las Palmas. The streets were narrow and cobbled, and the houses were painted ochre and blue and green, with heavy studded doors and iron lamps, and everywhere there were palm trees, and on every corner, it seemed to Alan, a little shrine with a candle in it.

The reception was in the main hall. There were waiters with trays of white wine and little plates of papas arrugadas, wrinkled potatoes in their salt crusts with red and green mojo sauce, and a string quartet in the corner playing Vivaldi to nobody, and about sixty people in summer clothes standing in clusters, talking at the tops of their voices. Archaeologists, Alan decided, could be identified by their shoes. Sponsors could be identified by their watches.

And all around them, in the old glass cases that lined the walls, the dead.

Alan had not been warned about this. The Museo Canario held one of the largest collections of pre-Hispanic remains in the world, and it did not believe in hiding them. There were shelves of skulls, row after row of them, the ancient islanders, the Canarios, staring out through the glass with polite, patient eyes. There were mummies wrapped in goatskin, curled on their sides like sleepers. There were pots, and clay seals stamped with spirals and triangles, and grinding stones, and the long bones of the arms and legs stacked like firewood.

It was like holding a cocktail party in a crypt.

Alan took a glass of wine and went to look at the skulls, because they were the only people in the room he wasn't expected to talk to.

"They're not frightening," said a voice beside him. "People think they're frightening. They're not. They're just waiting for us to pay attention."

The man was tall and thin and very still, with a long, deeply tanned face, silver hair swept back from a high forehead, and a short, neat grey beard. He was wearing a sand-coloured linen suit, beautifully cut, with an open-collared shirt and no tie, and a pair of reading glasses hung on a cord around his neck. At his throat, on a leather thong, was a small carved pendant of red clay: a seal, like the ones in the cases, with a pattern of triangles.

He had the darkest, most attentive eyes Alan had ever seen. When they looked at you, you felt looked at.

"You're Alan," said the man. "Vasco has told me nothing about you, which is how I know he's in love. Rayco Baute."

He held out his hand. His palm was rough, cracked across the pads of the fingers, with a thin white scar across the back. A digger's hand, in a sponsor's suit.

"He's told me a great deal about you," said Alan.

"Then I'm in trouble." Baute smiled. It was a warm smile and a tired one. "I've stolen him from you, I'm afraid. For the week. I'll give him back on Monday. He's the best reader of sacred light I've ever worked with, and I've worked with the best. He sees things the rest of us walk past."

"He does," said Alan, and found that he meant it, and that it hurt.

Baute was looking at the skulls again.

"When I was a boy," he said, "my grandmother used to take me up to the burial caves above her village. She'd tell me their names. Not the real ones. Nobody remembers the real ones. She gave them names, so they'd have names. 'This is Tamara. This is Guayasén. Say good morning.'" He touched the clay pendant at his throat, lightly, with two fingers. "There's a hotel there now. With a swimming pool. You'd like it. Everybody likes it."

Then someone called his name from across the hall, and he excused himself with a small bow, and was gone, and Alan was left with a glass of warm white wine and the feeling of having been told something important in a language he didn't speak.

---

Vasco was across the room, surrounded.

Alan watched him for a while. He was standing in front of a large photograph of the cave, all golden rock and shadow, explaining something with both hands, drawing the path of the light through the air in front of him. The people around him were listening. Really listening, the way people listen to someone who knows something they don't. A woman in pearls had her hand on her heart. A heavy-set man in a white guayabera shirt, with a gold watch and a cigar he wasn't allowed to light, was nodding along as if he understood every word.

In Lisbon, Vasco had been a priest's assistant, then a priest's historian, then a man who had left the priesthood's employ and didn't quite know what he was any more. He had been brilliant and shy and slightly lost.

He wasn't lost here.

Alan finished his wine and went outside.

There was a woman sitting on the museum steps, smoking.

She was in her early forties, compact and square-shouldered, with thick black hair cut short for convenience and a single streak of grey running back from her forehead like a lightning strike. She was wearing a man's linen shirt in faded rust, with the sleeves rolled up past the elbow, and khaki trousers, and scuffed desert boots. A big pair of tortoiseshell sunglasses was pushed up on top of her head, though the sun had gone down an hour ago. A set of car keys hung from a carabiner on her belt loop.

She was on her third attempt to light a cigarette with a lighter that didn't want to work. On the third attempt, it did.

Alan sat down on the step beside her.

"Can I have one of those?"

She looked at him. She looked at the jacket. She lowered the sunglasses from the top of her head to the bridge of her nose, and looked at him over the top of them, the way a customs officer looks at a suitcase.

"No," she said.

"Fair enough."

They sat in silence for a while. From inside came the sound of the string quartet and of Vasco's voice, rising with enthusiasm, and a burst of laughter.

"You're the boyfriend," said the woman eventually.

"I'm the boyfriend."

"The bookseller."

"Alan."

"Carmen Trujillo. I run the archive." She exhaled smoke at the street. "I don't do parties."

"You're at a party."

"I'm outside a party," she said. "There's a difference. You should know. You're outside it too."

Alan said nothing.

Carmen held out the packet. Coronas, the local brand, crushed almost flat. He took one, and she lit it for him, and it took her three tries.

"Your doctor's very good," she said. "Everybody says so. Rayco's in love with him. Not like that. Rayco's in love with anyone who sees what he sees."

"And what does he see?"

Carmen looked at him for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes, something tired and guarded, and then it was gone.

"Ask him," she said. She stood up, and ground her cigarette out on the step with the heel of her boot. "Welcome to Gran Canaria. Don't swim after dark."

"Why does everybody keep saying that?"

But she had already gone back inside.

---

It was after nine when they got back to the hotel, and Vasco was still talking.

He talked in the taxi, and in the lobby, past Nieves's eyebrow, and in the sneezing lift. He talked about Baute, who had found Risco Caído's hidden channel when he was thirty, and had spent the twenty-six years since trying to get the money to dig it properly. He talked about the dinner on Thursday, when he would present his first findings to the sponsors. He talked about the light.

Alan let him. It was like standing next to a fire.

"Let's walk," he said, when they reached the room. "I want to see the beach."

The promenade was full of people in the evening heat, families and couples and old men in vests walking their dogs. Further down the beach, on the sand, men were building something out of wooden pallets and old furniture and dried palm fronds: a great untidy pyramid, taller than a man.

"For San Juan," said Vasco. "Sunday night. The Noche de San Juan. They light bonfires all along the beach. The whole city comes down to the sea. You jump over the fires for luck, and at midnight you go into the water. Rayco says the beach looks like it's burning from end to end."

"Rayco says."

"Alan."

"I'm teasing." He wasn't, quite. "Will we be here for it?"

"I'll be on the mountain. The sunrise on the twenty-fourth is the whole point. It's the morning the light turns back." Vasco took his hand. "But you could come up. You could see it with me."

"I could," said Alan, and squeezed his hand, and didn't say yes.

They walked to the end of the promenade, where the beach ended in rocks and the lights of the city gave out, and stood at the rail looking at the sea.

The grey cloud above them had turned a dull bruised orange from the streetlights. The sea was black and quiet. The air was so warm and so heavy that it was like breathing through a damp cloth.

And then, out on the water, past the reef, Alan saw the mist.

At first he took it for a shadow. A low black line on the water, like a second horizon, a few hundred metres out. Then he saw that it was moving. It was rolling in towards the shore, low and thick, not like the high grey of the panza above, but black, oily black, hugging the surface of the sea like smoke from a burning ship.

It was coming in against the wind.

Alan could feel the wind on his face, the trade wind, steady and warm, blowing off the land and out to sea. It lifted the hair from his forehead. It flapped the awning of the café behind them. And the black mist was rolling in towards them against it, slowly, deliberately, the way a tide comes in.

It reached the reef. The waves breaking on the reef went quiet, all at once, as if someone had laid a hand over them.

It reached the sand.

And then the smell reached him.

Alan had smelled it once before in his life, eight months ago, in the dark, at the bottom of a cistern under Lisbon, up to his chest in black water, with a dead man's hand on his shoulder and something enormous in the dark beyond the light of his torch. Something that had looked at him, and known him for what he was, the blood of the men who had made the bells, and withdrawn.

Salt. Cold stone. Rust. Old, deep water that had never seen the sun.

He was holding the rail so tightly his knuckles had gone white.

"Alan?" Vasco was looking at him. "What is it?"

On the beach, a dog was barking at the mist. Its owner was laughing and pulling it away.

Alan opened his mouth.

He thought about Vasco's face in the museum, lit up, explaining the light to people who were listening. He thought about the word Thursday, and the word Rayco, and the photographs of the cave, all gold. He thought about the week Vasco was having, the best week of his life, perhaps, the week his new life began.

He thought about saying: I think it's back. I think it followed me. I think the thing from the cistern is here.

And he thought about Vasco's face if he said it. How the light would go out of it.

"Nothing," said Alan. "I'm tired. And I think I'm sunburnt in places I didn't know I had."

Vasco looked at him a moment longer. Then he smiled, and put his arm around Alan's shoulders, and turned him gently away from the sea.

Behind them, the mist lay along the waterline like something sleeping, and did not move again.

---

He called Inês again from the phone box at midnight, while Vasco was in the shower. It took all the rest of Chano's coins.

"It's here," he said. "It's come for me."

There was a long silence on the line from Lisbon.

"What's here?"

"You know what."

"Alan." Her voice had changed. "Are you sure?"

"I smelled it." He was looking at the beach, at the dark line along the sand. "I'd know that smell anywhere, Inês. I'll smell it on my deathbed."

"Have you told Vasco?"

He didn't answer.

"Alan. Have you told Vasco?"

"He's happy," said Alan. "He's so happy. It's his week. It's the first thing he's ever had that's just his. I can't walk into the middle of it and say, sorry, darling, I'm being haunted again."

"You're not being haunted. You're being sensible. Tell him."

"Tomorrow."

"Alan."

"Tomorrow," he said. "I promise. Find out what you can. Anything. Old papers. Anything about the Canaries."

"I have a thesis," said Inês.

"Then you'll want something to do instead," said Alan, and the line went dead.

---

When he got back to the room, Vasco was lying on the bed in a towel, reading his notebook and frowning at it, with his glasses on the end of his nose.

The balcony doors were open. The ceiling fan turned slowly overhead, stirring the warm air and doing nothing about it. From outside came the soft hush of the waves, back to normal now, as if nothing had happened.

Alan stood in the doorway in his borrowed jacket and his split trousers and looked at him.

Vasco looked up.

"You've been on the phone to Inês," he said.

"How do you know?"

"You've got your Inês face. Guilty, and a bit pleased with yourself." Vasco closed the notebook. "What did she say?"

"Pessoa was sick on the Eça de Queirós."

"The first edition?"

"The first edition."

Vasco considered this gravely. "He always did hate Eça."

Alan laughed. It came out of him too suddenly, too loud, like something escaping, and Vasco smiled, and held out his hand.

"Take off that terrible jacket," he said. "And come here."

Alan took off the terrible jacket.

It was easy. That was the thing about it. That was the thing he could never quite explain to Inês, or to himself, about the eight months. It was easy, and it was funny, and it was familiar, and familiar had always been the word that frightened him most.

Vasco knew exactly where the sunburn was, and was careful of it, and then, when Alan complained about being treated like an invalid, deliberately not careful of it. Alan knew exactly which spot under Vasco's left shoulder blade made him laugh, and which made him stop laughing. Halfway through, the lift down the corridor sneezed, very loudly, and they both lost it completely, and lay there shaking with laughter with their foreheads pressed together, and then stopped laughing, and didn't need to say anything.

Afterwards, Vasco fell asleep almost at once, the way he did, one arm thrown across Alan's chest like a claim.

Alan lay awake.

He listened to the sea through the open balcony doors. He listened to Vasco's breathing, slow and even. He listened for the other sound, the one under the waves, and couldn't tell if he heard it or not.

Eight months. A bed by the sea. A man who loved him, asleep with an arm across his chest, as if he might otherwise float away.

This was everything he had ever been afraid of.

Outside, on the dark beach, the bonfire stood unlit, waiting for Sunday.

## Chapter Two: The Island

*Wednesday, the nineteenth of June.*

Vasco left at dawn.

Alan half woke to the sound of him moving around the room in the grey light, trying to be quiet and failing, the way tall men always fail: the knock of a knee against the bedside table, a whispered curse in Portuguese, a boot dropped. Then a hand on his hair, and a kiss on the side of his head that smelled of toothpaste and sun cream.

"Coffee's by the bed," Vasco whispered. "It's terrible. It's from the machine in the lobby. Nieves made me promise not to tell anyone it exists."

"Mm," said Alan.

"I'll be back for dinner. Late. Go and see the island."

"Mm."

"Alan." The hand stayed on his hair a moment longer. "Are you all right? Last night, on the promenade. You looked like you'd seen a ghost."

This was it. This was the moment. Alan lay with his eyes closed, with the hand on his hair, and felt the words rising in him, ready. *I did see a ghost. I've seen this one before. It's the thing from the cistern, and it's here, and I'm frightened.*

"Sunburn," he said into the pillow. "I'm delicate."

Vasco laughed softly, and the hand went away, and the door closed.

Alan lay in the grey light and listened to the Land Rover start in the street below, and pull away, and fade into the sound of the sea.

Then he drank the terrible coffee, which was terrible, and went to see the island.

---

He went first to La Isleta, because Chano told him to.

"You want to see the real Las Palmas," said Chano, from behind his radio, "go to La Isleta. The tourists don't go. The fishermen are still there. My mother's family is from there. They'll rob you, but honestly."

La Isleta was a lump of old volcanic rock on the end of the city, joined to the rest of it by a thin neck of sand, with the port on one side of it and the open ocean on the other. It was all narrow streets of low, square houses painted in faded blues and pinks and greens, washing hung on every roof, dogs asleep in every doorway, and at the bottom of it a little harbour full of wooden fishing boats with names painted on their bows. *Virgen del Carmen. Dos Hermanos. Esperanza.* The air smelled of diesel and fish and frying oil.

On the quay, under the grey panza, an old woman was sorting a net.

She was small and square, built like a bollard, in a black dress and a faded apron and black rubber boots, with a black scarf tied over grey hair. Her face had been in the sun for seventy years and looked like it, brown and creased like a walnut. Beside her, on an upturned fish crate, sat a thin old man in a flat cap, mending the same net, with an unlit cigarette in the corner of his mouth.

The old woman was pulling things out of the net and laying them on the quay. Not fish. Alan stopped to look.

A clay pot, cracked and furred with weed. A round iron ball the size of a grapefruit, so rusted it had grown a skin of orange coral. A short, thick bolt of iron, flanged at one end. And a helmet.

It was a soldier's helmet, the old kind, the kind Alan had seen in museums and in paintings of conquistadors: a round steel cap with a brim, and a ridge running over the top of it like the crest of a cockerel. It was so corroded that it had gone the colour of dried blood. Weed hung from it. A small crab was living in it.

"That's old," said Alan, in Spanish.

The old woman looked up at him. Her eyes were very black and very sharp.

"Fourteen seventy-eight," she said. "The year the Castilians came. My grandson took it to the museum last week and they told him. Fourteen seventy-eight. Five hundred years in the sea, and it comes up in my net on a Tuesday." She tipped the crab gently out of the helmet and back into the harbour. "Every day this week. Every day, something. Cannonballs. Crossbow bolts. Pots. A sword on Saturday, all in one piece. The sea's emptying its pockets."

"Why?"

The old man on the crate took the cigarette out of his mouth, looked at it, and put it back in.

"Because it's looking for something," said the old woman. "When you lose something, you empty your pockets." She held out a weathered hand. "Pino. Doña Pino, to you. That's Lolo. He doesn't talk."

"I talk," said the old man.

"He doesn't talk to tourists."

"I'm not a tourist," said Alan. "I'm Portuguese."

Doña Pino looked at him for a long moment, at his sunburn and his split trousers, which he had sewn up badly with the hotel sewing kit.

"That's worse," she said. "The Portuguese think they own the sea." But she was almost smiling. She held up the helmet, and turned it in her hands, and looked into it as if it were a bowl of water she could read. "You know what the old people said, here, when I was a girl? They said the sea has a memory. It never forgets anything it's taken. And it never forgets anything that was taken back from it."

She set the helmet down on the quay, very carefully, as if it might still have a head in it.

"It's been very quiet for a long time, the sea here," she said. "Five hundred years. Now it's restless. I don't like it."

Across the harbour, a big grey salvage boat was tied up at the commercial quay, all cranes and winches, with a name painted on her stern in white letters: *ATLANTIC RECOVERY, Pell Marine, Nassau.* Two men in orange overalls were sitting on her deck, smoking, watching the fishing boats.

Alan didn't notice it. Nobody did.

---

He walked back through the city along the beach, in the grey heat, with his shirt sticking to his back, and somewhere around the middle of Las Canteras he realised he had made up his mind.

He would tell Vasco tonight. Properly. He would sit him down, and pour him a drink, and say it plainly.

And before that, he would find out what he could. So that when he said it, it would sound less like a man who was frightened of the sea, and more like a man who knew something.

It was the kind of decision that felt brave at the time.

---

He went to the cathedral, because cathedrals were where bells were, and bells, in Alan's experience, were where trouble was.

The Cathedral of Santa Ana stood on its own square in Vegueta, an enormous grey stone front with two square towers, facing a long plaza of palm trees and cobbles and, for reasons nobody could ever explain to him, eight life-sized bronze dogs. Inside it was dark and cool and smelled of candle wax and old stone. A few old women were praying. A few tourists were reading their guidebooks in whispers.

And somewhere above him, a clock was failing to strike.

He could hear it. A heavy, grinding, catching sound, high in the north tower, like a great iron throat trying to clear itself, and then silence, and then the grinding again.

A very small, very old man in a long black coat was standing at the foot of the tower stairs, glaring up at the noise through a pair of glasses as thick as the bottoms of bottles. He was holding an iron ring the size of a dinner plate, hung with at least forty keys.

"It's stuck," said Alan.

The old man turned the glasses on him.

"It has been stuck," he said, in careful Spanish, "since Sunday. The man who winds it is in hospital with his gallbladder. The man who replaces the man who winds it is on holiday in Fuerteventura. The bishop says, Fermín, wind it yourself. Fermín is seventy-six years old and has a heart like a broken accordion." He glared back up the stairs. "The bishop is not seventy-six."

"I could try," said Alan.

"You know clocks?"

"I know bells." Alan heard himself say it, and was surprised. "My family made them. A long time ago."

The old man looked at him for a long moment through the glasses. Then he held out the ring of keys.

---

The bell chamber was at the top of a hundred and twelve stone steps, which Don Fermín climbed behind him one at a time, stopping on every landing to tell Alan that he would die on the next one.

It was a square stone room open to the sky on four sides through tall arched windows, with heavy black beams across the ceiling and pigeon feathers everywhere. Through the windows Alan could see the whole of the old town laid out below him, the red-tiled roofs and the little courtyards and the palm trees, and beyond them the grey sea, and above it all the flat grey lid of the panza.

In the middle of the room, on a cradle of iron and oak, hung the clock's mechanism: a great black box of wheels and gears and ratchets, and two long chains going down through the floor to the weights. And beyond it, in the largest of the arches, the bells.

There were four of them. Alan went to the clock first, because he'd promised. It took him twenty minutes, a great deal of swearing, and the loss of most of the skin on his left hand. Something in the escapement had slipped a tooth, and when he worked out what it was and eased it back, the whole black box shuddered, and sighed, and began to tick again, slowly, like a heart starting.

Don Fermín, who had been sitting on a wooden chest in the corner getting his breath back, took off his glasses and polished them.

"Founder's hands," he said.

"What?"

"You have founder's hands. My grandfather said you could always tell. The men who make bells understand the things that hang." He put the glasses back on and looked at Alan very hard. "What did you say your name was?"

"Sanches. Alan Sanches."

The old man was very still for a moment. Then he got up off the chest, slowly, and went to the largest of the four bells, and beckoned.

It was a big bell, bigger than a man, dark bronze gone green at the lip. Around its shoulder ran a band of raised letters, and below that a frieze of leaves and shells, and below that, almost hidden in the shadow of the waist, a small cast mark: a shield, and on the shield a bell, and around the bell a ring of tiny letters.

Alan knew it before he could read it. He had seen it on a bell in Lisbon, at the bottom of a flooded tower, eight months ago. He had seen it on the frontispiece of his grandfather's books. He had seen it in his dreams.

*SANCHES ME FEZ. LISBOA. 1751.*

Sanches made me.

"The founders came from Lisbon," said Don Fermín softly. "In seventeen fifty-one. Three of them, on a ship from Lisbon, with a bell in the hold for Santa Ana. A gift, they said. They hung it here with their own hands. And then they went up into the mountains for a month." He looked at Alan. "My grandfather's grandfather was sacristan then. He wrote it down. He said it was the strangest thing. Bell founders, from Lisbon. And they came to look at the mountain, not the sea."

Alan put his hand on the bronze. It was cold, and it hummed faintly under his palm, the way bells always do, as if remembering the last time they were struck.

"What were they looking for?"

Don Fermín didn't answer at once. He went back to the wooden chest he'd been sitting on, and lifted the lid, and rummaged among old vestments and candle stubs and a broken crucifix, and came up with a flat packet of oilcloth, tied with a faded cord, and sealed with a lump of black wax the size of a coin.

Pressed into the wax was the same mark. The shield and the bell.

"They left this," said the old man. "With my family. 'For the next founder who comes.' That's what it says on the outside. Two hundred and forty-five years we've kept it. My father said, Fermín, nobody will ever come. My father was wrong about many things."

He held it out.

Alan took it. It was light, and dry, and it felt heavier than the bell.

---

He opened it in the plaza, sitting on the edge of a fountain between two of the bronze dogs, with his skinned hand stinging.

Inside the oilcloth were four sheets of thick paper, folded together, covered on both sides in a small, cramped, brown handwriting that had faded in places almost to nothing. It was Portuguese, but the Portuguese of two and a half centuries ago, with long s's like f's and abbreviations he didn't know and whole words that had worn away at the folds.

He could read the first line.

*Aos que vierem depois de nós.* To those who come after us.

And further down, a word that made the back of his neck go cold despite the heat. *Mar.* The Sea. With a capital letter.

He sat there for a long time.

Then he went and found a copy shop in Triana with a fax machine, and paid an outrageous sum to send all eight pages to a bookshop in Lisbon, along with a careful drawing of the founder's mark on the bell, and on the cover sheet he wrote: *Inês. Sorry. Can you read this? It's urgent. A.*

---

It was the middle of the afternoon when he got to the museum, and the courtyard was full of crates.

A van was backed up to the side door, a big white van with a company name on the side in gold letters, MONTESDEOCA HOTELES S.A., and two men in overalls were loading wooden crates into it from a trolley. The crates were long and flat and heavy, and each one had a sheet of paper stapled to the side.

Alan slowed down as he passed. He read the nearest sheet.

It was a list, typed, with the museum's crest at the top. *Conservation transfer. Risco Caído, Cave 6.* And underneath, a column of items, each with a date.

*Obsidian disc, polished, 1 of 7. Removed for conservation.*

*Obsidian disc, polished, 2 of 7. Removed for conservation. 19 June.*

And below that, more lines, with the dates typed in already, one for each day of the week, with blank spaces waiting.

One a day.

The man with the trolley saw him reading and moved between him and the crate, not unfriendly, but firmly. "Display cases," he said. "New ones. From Don Ignacio. A gift for the museum."

"And the discs?"

The man shrugged. "I just carry them."

---

He found Carmen in the archive.

It was in the basement of the museum, a long low room with a vaulted ceiling, full from floor to ceiling with grey metal shelving and grey cardboard boxes, and it smelled of dust and old paper and cigarette smoke, though there was a large sign on the wall that said PROHIBIDO FUMAR. Carmen was sitting under the sign, smoking, at a desk buried in files, with a typewriter and a fax machine and an ashtray that had not been emptied since the Franco era.

She looked up as he came in, and lowered the sunglasses from her head to the end of her nose.

"No," she said.

"I haven't asked anything."

"You were going to."

"The discs from the cave," said Alan. "The obsidian discs. There's a list upstairs. One a day. They're being taken out, one every day. Why?"

"Conservation."

"Of what? They've been there five hundred years."

"That's why they need conserving." She went back to her file.

"Carmen." He sat down on the edge of her desk, which she clearly hated. "Last night on the beach there was a mist. A black mist. It came in off the sea against the wind. It smelled of salt, and something else. Something old. I've seen it before. I've smelled it before, in Lisbon, and when I smelled it there, people died."

She didn't look up from the file. But her hand, holding the pen, had stopped moving.

"And today," said Alan, "I watched an old woman in La Isleta pull a conquistador's helmet out of her net, and she told me the sea's been giving things back all week. Things it's kept for five hundred years. And at the same time, somebody is taking apart the cave, one piece a day, and calling it conservation."

Carmen put the pen down.

"It's June," she said. "It's the panza de burro. Every June the cloud comes down, and the tourists see shapes in it. Every June the fishermen pull up junk, because the currents change. Every June someone comes into my archive with a theory." She stubbed her cigarette out, too hard. "Go to the beach, Señor Sanches. Swim. In the daytime. Go and buy your boyfriend a present. It's a beautiful island. Enjoy it."

She said it too fast. She was already turning away from him as she said it, turning her whole chair round to the shelves behind her, so that by the time she finished she had her back to him, and was pulling a box off a shelf that she didn't need.

Alan looked at the back of her head for a long moment.

On the corner of her desk, under the ashtray, there was a photograph in a cheap plastic frame, turned face down.

He didn't touch it.

"Thank you," he said, and turned to go.

"Señor Sanches."

She still had her back to him. Without turning round, she reached out, took a thick old book off the shelf in front of her, and held it out behind her at arm's length, the way you'd hand someone something you didn't want to be seen giving.

It was a fat brown volume with a cracked spine. *Historia de la conquista de las siete islas de Canaria.* Fray Juan de Abreu Galindo, written in the fifteen-nineties.

"If you want to understand this island," said Carmen, to the shelves, "start with the people it belonged to. Read the part about the harimaguadas." A pause. "Bring it back. It's the archive's copy. If you get sand in it, I'll have you deported."

He took it, and left.

When he had gone, Carmen Trujillo sat with her back to the door for a long time. Then she turned round and righted the photograph and looked at it: two women on a mountain, squinting into the sun, one with a pencil through her hair.

At home, in her flat, on the table by the door, there was an answering machine with a message on it that she had listened to only once, on the morning of the twenty-second of December, and had never been able to listen to again, and had never been able to erase.

*It's a door, Carmen. And someone's opening it.*

She lit another cigarette. It took her three tries.

---

Chano was waiting for him at the kiosk with a newspaper and a theory.

"You've been to the museum," he said. "You saw Montesdeoca's van."

"How do you —"

"Everyone saw Montesdeoca's van. Montesdeoca's van is very big." Chano lowered his voice. "You know what he wants? He wants to build a hotel in Artenara. A big one. Guanche-themed. With a cave restaurant. Waiters in goatskins. You understand? And the only thing in his way is that dig. Because if the cave's important, he can't build. But if somebody proves the cave's not important…"

He let it hang in the air, and sold a woman a lottery ticket.

Alan stood by the phone box with his newspaper and thought about a man in a white guayabera with a gold watch and a cigar he wasn't allowed to light, nodding along as Vasco explained the light, as if he understood every word.

It made sense. It was neat. It was the kind of explanation that a man who was frightened of something much larger would very much like to be true.

---

Vasco didn't get back until after ten.

He came in grey with dust and exhaustion, and kissed Alan, and fell face down across the bed with his boots still on, and said into the counterpane, "I love it. I love it so much. My back is broken."

Alan sat on the edge of the bed and unlaced his boots for him.

"They took another disc out today," said Vasco, muffled. "Rayco's sending them all down to the city for conservation. One a day. He wants them stabilised before the big public opening in the autumn. He's right, they're very fragile, obsidian splits along the grain. But I hate it. I hate seeing the empty sockets. It's like a smile with teeth missing."

Alan put the boots side by side on the floor.

This was it. He had decided. He would say it now.

"Vasco," he said.

But Vasco was asleep.

Alan sat there for a while in the light of the bedside lamp, looking at him. Then he took off the rest of Vasco's clothes, as gently as he could, the way you'd undress a child who'd fallen asleep in the car, and pulled the sheet over him, and turned out the light.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow, at the dinner. After his presentation. When he's happy.

It was the kind of decision that felt kind at the time.

---

On the other side of the city, in La Isleta, in a small blue house three streets back from the harbour, a fisherman called Pepe Santana went to bed at half past ten, as he had every night for fifty years.

He was sixty-four. He had been at sea since he was twelve. In 1971 he had been one of the crew of a trawler called the *Virgen del Pino*, which had gone down in a storm off the African coast with eleven men aboard, and he had been one of the four who had been pulled out of the water alive the next morning, clinging to a hatch cover, so cold that he couldn't speak for a week.

He had never talked about it. Not to his wife, while she lived, and not to his sons. He had gone back to sea a month later, because what else was there, and he had never once since then been able to sleep with the window open.

Tonight the window was shut, as always. The door was locked. The house was quiet and dry and warm.

He woke at two in the morning to the smell of salt.

He lay in the dark with his heart hammering, and listened, and heard it, very close, as close as the pillow under his ear. The slow rush and draw of waves.

Then he felt the cold, rising from the foot of the bed.

Pepe Santana did not shout. He was a fisherman. He knew the sea, and he knew, at once, with the whole of his body, that the sea had come back for what had been taken from it, twenty-five years ago, on a cold morning off the coast of Africa, when a fishing boat out of Agadir had hauled him out of the water by his collar.

He only said, very quietly, into the dark:

"Ah. There you are."

In the morning his son found him in his bed, under the dry sheets, with the window closed and the door locked, and his lungs full of the sea.

## Chapter Three: The Break-in

*Thursday, the twentieth of June.*

Chano told him about Pepe Santana, because Chano told everyone everything.

"Drowned," he said, folding a newspaper for a customer without looking at it. "In his bed. In La Isleta. Sixty-four years old, fifty years at sea, never so much as a cold, and he drowns in his own bed with the window shut." He crossed himself with the newspaper. "His son found him. The doctor said heart. Heart, with his lungs full of seawater. My cousin's wife's sister lives next door. She heard the son screaming."

Alan stood at the kiosk with his coins in his hand and felt the morning heat go cold on his skin.

"Where in La Isleta?"

"Calle Pescadores. Near the harbour. Why?"

But Alan was already walking.

---

Doña Pino was not on the quay. Don Lolo was, alone on his upturned crate, mending the same net, with the same unlit cigarette, and when Alan asked where she was he took the cigarette out of his mouth and pointed with it up the hill, to a little white church with a tin bell.

She was sitting in the back pew in her black dress, with a missal open on her knee.

She didn't look up when Alan sat down beside her. She was looking at the inside cover of the missal, where someone had written, long ago, in blue ink gone purple with age, four names in a column.

*José Santana Rivero.*

*Tomás Martel.*

*Juan Medina.*

*Pino Suárez Ojeda.*

The first and the third had a line drawn through them. The line through Juan Medina was old and faded. The line through José Santana was black and new and the ink had gone through the page.

"The *Virgen del Pino*," said Doña Pino. "Nineteen seventy-one. Eleven men and one woman, because the cook was sick and I went in his place, for the money, because Lolo had broken his arm. A storm off Cape Bojador. She went down in four minutes. We were in the water all night." She touched the names with one brown finger, one after another. "Four of us came up in the morning. Pepe. Tomás, the boy, he was fifteen; he lives in Venezuela now, God keep him there. Juan Medina. And me. Juan died in nineteen eighty, of drink. That's not the sea. That's just Juan."

She touched the last name. Her own.

"Pepe," she said, "is the sea."

"Doña Pino —"

"You know what the old women said, when I was a girl?" She closed the missal. "They said the sea never forgets a debt. It gives you back, sometimes, if it's in a good mood, or it's busy, or it isn't hungry. But it writes you down. And one day it comes to collect."

She looked at him then, properly, with her black, sharp eyes.

"You know something about this," she said. "I saw it on Tuesday. You looked at my helmet like it was a letter addressed to you."

Alan sat in the cool of the little church and thought of the cistern under Lisbon. The black water at his chest. The thing in the dark beyond his torch, enormous, patient, considering him. Knowing him, somehow, for the blood of the bell-makers. And then, as if it had decided something, withdrawing.

It had let him go. And now it was here.

"Yes," he said. "I think I do."

---

He went to the archive with Abreu Galindo under his arm.

He had sat up half the night with it, on the hotel balcony, while Vasco slept. Carmen was under the PROHIBIDO FUMAR sign, smoking, exactly where he had left her. She looked at the book, and then at him.

"You read it."

"Most of it. Your friar is very rude about the Portuguese."

"He was a Franciscan. He was rude about everyone." She didn't tell him to go away. That was new. She tipped her chin at the chair on the other side of the desk, and after a moment he sat in it.

"The harimaguadas," he said. "Tell me about them. Properly. Not the friar's version."

She looked at him for a long moment over the sunglasses. Then she told him.

They were the island's own priestesses, she said. Young unmarried women, living apart, in sacred enclosures, on the cliffs and in the mountains. They bathed in the sea at dawn, for the island's sake. They kept the rites for rain, and for the sun, and for the dead. The Spanish chroniclers called them vestals, because that was the only word they had. When the island fell, they vanish from every record. Not killed, not baptised, not married off. Just gone, between one page and the next.

"And when did it fall?" said Alan.

"It took five years. People think it was quick. It wasn't." She counted it off on her fingers, bored and precise, like a woman reciting her own address. "June 1478, Juan Rejón lands at the mouth of the Guiniguada and builds a stockade, on Saint John's day. That's your old lady's helmet. Then five years of war in the mountains. In April 1483 the last of our people are up at Ansite, in the south. Bentejuí, the last war chief, and the faycán of Telde, the high priest, walk to the edge of a cliff together and throw themselves off it rather than surrender. Atis Tirma, they shouted. For my land. The rest come down and are baptised. End of story."

"Is there a story about the priestesses and the sea?" said Alan. "At the end?"

Carmen went very still.

"Who told you that?"

"An old woman in La Isleta. Sort of."

She looked at the face-down photograph on the corner of her desk. Then she lit another cigarette from the end of the first.

"There's a story," she said. "Rayco used to tell it. Dr. Baute. He taught me, twenty years ago, at the university. He'd tell it at the end of term, with the lights off." She blew smoke at the ceiling. "That in the last spring of the war, when the Castilian ships came round to the north coast, the harimaguadas went down to the shore at night and called the sea up against them. And it came. And it didn't stop. It took the ships, and then the coast, and then our own villages. And the women went up into the mountains, to a cave where the sun comes in through the roof, and shut it out again. With the light."

"Risco Caído," said Alan.

"Where your boyfriend is spending his week, yes." She tapped ash. "It's a fairy tale. It's what you tell students when you want them to feel something about five hundred years ago. The old women in Artenara used to say the cave holds the sea down. My grandmother said it. Everybody's grandmother said it."

"Do you believe it?"

"I'm an archivist. I believe in documents."

"Then where are the documents?"

"Not here. We have bones and pots. The Castilians kept the paper." She jerked her head towards the wall, towards the cathedral. "Casa de Colón. Behind Santa Ana. Ask for Mercedes Quintana. She'll hate you. She hates everyone who isn't dead."

"And Montesdeoca? The van?"

Her mouth went thin. "Ignacio Montesdeoca has built half the south coast on top of our dead, and now he gives the museum display cases. Don't get me started on Ignacio Montesdeoca."

Alan told her about Pepe Santana then. The bed, the closed window, the sea in his lungs. The list in Doña Pino's missal.

Carmen listened without moving. When he had finished, she sat for a long time looking at nothing. Her eyes went, once, to a clipboard hanging on a nail by the door: the conservation list, with its column of dates. Wednesday. Today. Tomorrow.

Then she stubbed her cigarette out and stood up.

"I have work," she said. "Go away, Señor Sanches."

But she said it quietly, and she didn't turn her back.

---

He called Inês from Chano's phone box, because he couldn't not.

"I haven't finished the letter," she said, before he could speak. "It's two hundred and forty years old and someone wrote it with a chicken. But I've found something else."

"Where?"

"Your grandfather's boxes. In the back room. The ones you never open." Paper rustled. "There's a ledger. The foundry's accounts, seventeen-forty to seventeen-sixty. Bells cast, bells sold, bronze bought. And in seventeen fifty-one, a voyage. Passage for three, to the Canaries, and a bell in the hold. And beside it, in the margin, in the same hand as your letter: *para estudar a fechadura.* To study the lock."

Alan leaned his forehead against the glass.

"And in seventeen fifty-five," said Inês, "after the earthquake, after the wave, there's a page with no figures on it at all. Just a sentence. *Fizemos o que aprendemos nas Canárias. O Mar está calado.* We did what we learned in the Canaries. The Sea is silent."

"They shut it," said Alan. "In Lisbon. With what they learned here."

"They shut it," said Inês. "Which means it can be shut. Which means it knows the people who shut it." A pause. "Alan. Your blood. That's why it backed off you in the cistern. It knew you."

"And here?"

Inês was quiet for a long time.

"Here," she said, "I'd keep away from the water."

---

Casa de Colón was two minutes' walk from the cathedral, down a narrow street of old ochre houses: the old governors' palace, where Columbus was supposed to have stayed in 1492 while they mended the *Pinta*'s rudder. It had a stone doorway carved like lace, and inside, one courtyard opening onto another, with dark wooden balconies all round and palm trees in the middle and, for reasons nobody had ever explained to Alan's satisfaction, two large green parrots in a cage, who watched him come in and said something rude in Spanish.

Mercedes Quintana was in the library on the first floor, at a long table under a window, with a magnifying glass in one hand and a pencil in the other. She was in her sixties, small and straight-backed, in a grey cardigan in thirty-degree heat, with her grey hair in a bun and a pair of half-moon glasses on a chain. She looked at him over them exactly the way Carmen did. He wondered if they taught it at the university.

"Carmen Trujillo sent me," said Alan.

"Then you have my sympathy," said Mercedes Quintana, "but not my time."

"She said you'd hate me."

"She's usually right." But she put the magnifying glass down. "What do you want?"

"Fourteen eighty-three. The end of the conquest. I've heard the Canario story. Something about the priestesses calling the sea." He hesitated. "I want to know what the Castilians saw."

She looked at him for a long, measuring moment. Then she got up without a word and went to a tall cabinet at the end of the room, and unlocked it with a key from her cardigan pocket, and came back with a box. Inside the box, between sheets of tissue, was a thick bundle of paper, brown and spotted, tied with a ribbon that had once been red.

"*Información de testigos*," she said. "Fourteen eighty-four. An inquiry, by order of the Catholic Monarchs, into the loss of three of the Crown's ships and the royal camp on the north coast, the year before. Sworn statements. Sailors, soldiers, a priest. Nobody has asked to see it in eleven years." She untied the ribbon with small, exact fingers. "Most historians think it's an embarrassment. They prefer the battles."

She turned the pages slowly, and read aloud, translating the old Castilian as she went in a dry, level voice, as if she were reading a shopping list.

"'The third witness, Juan de Quintana, master of the caravel *Santa Clara*, being sworn, said: that on the night of the twentieth of March, being the eve of the equinox, with the ships at anchor off Gáldar and no wind, the sea rose. That it rose black, black as pitch, and came against the wind, which was blowing off the land. That it took the *San Pedro* at her anchor with all her people, and then the *Santa Clara*, from which this witness was thrown, and then the *Magdalena*. That it came up over the shore and into the royal camp, and drowned men in their tents who had not been near the water. That it went on into the villages of the heathen, and drowned them also, and this witness heard their women screaming. And that on the cliffs above Gáldar, by the light of the fires, this witness saw women in white, standing in a line, singing.'"

She turned a page.

"'Asked how long the sea continued, he said: that it came every night thereafter, for three months, into the low places, and into houses, and into the beds of men, and took them, and no priest could stop it, though many masses were said. And that on the morning of the blessed Saint John the Baptist, the twenty-fourth of June, at the rising of the sun, the sea went back, and has not risen since.'"

She closed the bundle.

"The bishop declared it a miracle of the Baptist," she said. "That is why, every year, on the night of San Juan, this whole city goes down to the sea and walks into the water at midnight. To thank the saint for sending it back. Five hundred years. Nobody remembers. They just do it."

Alan sat very still.

"Juan de Quintana," he said.

"My ancestor. Yes." She took off her glasses and polished them on her cardigan. "He survived the *Santa Clara*. A Canario fisherman pulled him out of the water at Gáldar. A heathen, saving a Castilian, in the middle of a war. He wrote that down too." She put the glasses back on. "He drowned in his own bed in Real de Las Palmas eleven days later. With the window shut. The family has always said it was drink."

She opened the bundle again at the last page, and turned it towards him, and put one finger under a few lines of small, cramped writing in the margin, in a different ink.

"The notary," she said. "He wrote this at the end, after all the witnesses. It isn't part of the record. I don't think he meant anyone to read it."

Alan leaned over and read it himself. The Castilian was old but the words were simple.

*Nadie preguntó a las mujeres.*

Nobody asked the women.

"In fifteen-oh-five," said Mercedes Quintana, "when the Holy Office set up its tribunal here, the first thing the inquisitors asked for was this file. They wanted the witches of eighty-three. They searched the mountains for a year." She tied the ribbon again, very neatly. "They found nobody. There was nobody left to find."

She put the bundle back in its box, and the box back in the cabinet, and locked it.

"Now," she said. "Why does a Portuguese bookseller want to know how the sea took the Crown's ships five hundred years ago?"

Alan thought of Pepe Santana in his bed in La Isleta, with the window shut. Pulled out of the sea off Africa by a fishing boat, twenty-five years ago.

"Because it's doing it again," he said.

Mercedes Quintana looked at him over her glasses for a long time. Then she sat down at her table, and picked up her magnifying glass, and bent over her work.

"Then I would stay out of the low places, Señor Sanches," she said, without looking up. "And I would be very careful on Saint John's night."

In the courtyard below, one of the parrots laughed.

---

He walked back along Las Canteras at noon, in the grey, damp heat, and he could not stop looking at the sea.

The tide was wrong.

He wouldn't have noticed on Tuesday. But there was a flight of stone steps going down from the promenade to the sand in the middle of the beach, and on Tuesday evening, when the tide was high, the water had reached the bottom step. Today it was noon, and the tide should have been out, the guidebook in the hotel lobby said so, a table of times in the back, and instead the water was lapping at the third step, flat and grey and calm and wrong.

A family was standing on the second step with their towels, staring at it.

The father was laughing. "Typical," he said, in English. "Fly two thousand miles for a beach, and they've put the sea on it."

He was very pink about the shoulders, and was wearing a pair of Union Jack shorts and white socks with his sandals, and holding a camcorder to his eye, filming the water. Beside him a woman in a straw hat and a floral sundress was rubbing sun cream into her own forearm with grim determination.

"Derek," she said. "Stop filming the sea. It's just the sea."

"It's coming up the steps, Gail."

"It's the tide."

"It's not supposed to be in. I checked." He swung the camcorder round and pointed it at Alan, delighted. "Is it, mate? Is this normal?"

"No," said Alan. "It isn't."

Derek lowered the camcorder, looking at him. Something in Alan's voice had got through.

"Well," he said, uncertainly. "Lucky we're at the dinner tonight, then. Out of the way."

"The dinner?"

"At the museum. Mister Montesdeoca invited us." Gail had brightened up. "He owns our hotel. He's ever so nice. He came round all the tables at breakfast. He said there's going to be a talk about the cave, and some old bones."

"There's a Portuguese doctor doing the talk," said Derek. "Supposed to be very clever."

"He is," said Alan.

And he went back to the hotel to try to find a jacket.

---

He had a plan. It wasn't a very good plan, but he had it the way you have a fever.

It went like this. Somebody was taking the cave apart, one disc a day. The discs were going somewhere in Montesdeoca's van. Montesdeoca wanted to build a hotel at Artenara, and needed the cave to be worthless. The mist had come, and the tide was wrong, and an old fisherman had drowned in his bed, and Alan knew, the way he had known it in Lisbon, that these things were connected, even if he could not see the threads.

And somewhere there would be paper. There was always paper. Sponsors signed contracts. Contracts had clauses. If Montesdeoca was paying for the dig, there would be a file, and if there was a file, it would be in Baute's office, in the museum, one floor up from the hall where the dinner would be, where everyone would be busy for at least two hours listening to Vasco talk about the light.

He would find the contract. He would find the clause. And then he would go to Vasco with it, and say: *I know what's happening. Here's the proof.* And it would not be a frightened man's ghost story. It would be a fact. A thing Vasco could hold in his hands.

He did not, at any point, consider simply telling Vasco what he had seen and asking for his help.

He noticed that he didn't consider it. He noticed it the way you notice a stone in your shoe, and walk on.

---

His suitcase had arrived. It was standing in the middle of the lobby when he came in, with a label on the handle that said MADRID in red, and on top of it, folded with great precision, was the borrowed navy jacket from the lost and found.

Nieves looked at him from behind the desk.

"Your luggage," she said. "And I have taken back the jacket. You will not need it any more." She paused. "No one needs that jacket."

He changed into the cream linen suit. It was creased from Madrid, and smelled faintly of the port, which had leaked a little, but it was his, and it fitted, and when he looked at himself in the mirror he looked, for the first time since he'd landed, like a man who knew what he was doing.

This was a lie, but it was a well-cut one.

---

The hall of the Museo Canario had been transformed. There were round tables with white cloths and candles, under the gaze of the skulls. There was a lectern, and a screen, and a slide projector humming on a trolley. There were waiters in white jackets, and the string quartet was back, playing Boccherini now, and about eighty people in their best summer clothes were finding their names on little cards.

Vasco was at the lectern with a stack of notes, checking the slides, too nervous to eat.

Alan went up and kissed him on the cheek.

"You look wonderful," said Vasco, distracted, and then looked properly, and smiled. "Your suit came."

"My suit came."

"I've missed your suit." He straightened Alan's collar. "Don't let me talk too long. If I go over twenty minutes, cough."

"I'll cough."

"And Alan." Vasco's hand stayed on his collar. "Thank you for being here. I know it's been boring for you. I know I've been… gone. Tonight's the last big thing. After tonight, I'll be better. I promise."

Alan looked at him, at the nervous, happy, exhausted face, and felt the plan sitting in his chest like something swallowed.

He could still not do it. He could sit at table seven, between a German professor and the woman in pearls, and listen to Vasco talk about the light, and cough at twenty minutes, and go home, and tell him. Just tell him.

"Break a leg," said Alan, and went to find his seat.

---

Vasco began at half past nine.

He was good. Alan had known he would be, but he hadn't known how good. Once he started, the nerves fell away. He stood at the lectern in his one good jacket with the slides glowing behind him, gold rock and shadow, and he talked about the light the way other men talk about women.

He told them how the people of the island, the Canarios, had cut a chamber into the soft volcanic rock of a cliff, high in the mountains above the clouds. How they had carved its walls with triangles, dozens of them, pointing down. How they had cut a narrow channel through the roof, so that on certain days, at certain hours, the sun came through it in a single blade of light and walked across the wall, touching the triangles one by one, like a finger running along a line of text.

"They built a calendar," he said. "That's what the textbooks will tell you. And it is a calendar. But I don't think it's only a calendar. I've spent my whole life in churches built to face the sunrise. I know what it feels like to stand in a building that was made to receive something. This is one of those buildings. This is a place where people waited for the sun to say something."

The room was very quiet.

At table four, Ignacio Montesdeoca, heavy and tanned in his white guayabera, nodded slowly, and turned his unlit cigar in his fingers.

At the head table, Rayco Baute sat with his hands folded and his eyes on Vasco, and on his face was an expression Alan didn't understand. It was pride, and it was love, and underneath them both, something else, something that looked almost like grief.

Alan looked at his watch. Nine forty.

At the side of the room, by the door that led to the back stairs, a museum guard in a grey uniform was sitting on a hard chair with his chin on his chest.

At table eleven, Derek was filming. He had been filming since the start, the camcorder held up in front of his face, the little red light glowing, and the woman beside him had complained twice, and a waiter had asked him once, politely, to stop. Now Derek wanted a better angle. He stood up, and edged out between the tables, and began to work his way round the edge of the room towards the front, crouching low, with Gail hissing his name behind him.

He walked straight into the guard's chair.

The guard woke up with a shout. The chair went over. Derek went over the chair. The camcorder flew out of his hands and skidded across the floor under table nine, and Derek went after it on his hands and knees, apologising in loud, slow English to the legs of the guests, and the guard went after Derek, and three waiters went after the guard, and for a moment every head in the room was turned to the far corner.

Alan stood up and walked out of the side door.

---

The back stairs were dark and smelled of floor polish. He went up them two at a time, with his heart going like a drum, past a landing and a fire extinguisher and a framed photograph of a nineteenth-century doctor with magnificent whiskers, and along a corridor of closed doors with names on them.

DR. R. BAUTE. DIRECTOR DE EXCAVACIONES.

The door was locked. It was an old lock, a museum lock, and Alan had spent his teenage years in Lisbon with a set of lock picks and very poor judgement. He had it open in under a minute, with a hairpin he had stolen from the woman in pearls, and he was proud of that for exactly as long as it took him to step inside and close the door behind him.

The office was dark. Street light came in through a tall window, orange and dim. There was a big wooden desk, piled with reports. A green-shaded banker's lamp. Shelves of books and boxes. A filing cabinet. On the wall, a long map of Gran Canaria.

He went to the filing cabinet first. The sponsor files were in the second drawer, in alphabetical order, and he found MONTESDEOCA HOTELES S.A. and pulled it out and spread it on the desk under the lamp, his hands not quite steady.

It was a sponsorship agreement. Twelve pages. Money for the dig, money for the conservation, money for the new display cases. In return, Montesdeoca's name on a plaque in the museum, and on the expedition's report, and on a little card on every table at tonight's dinner.

That was all.

He read it twice. He read the small print. He looked for a clause about Artenara, about hotels, about the cave being declared unimportant. There wasn't one. It was the most boring document he had ever read.

Alan stood there in the orange light with twelve pages of nothing, and felt the plan begin to slide out from under him.

Then he looked up, and saw the photograph.

It was on the wall above the desk, in a plain black frame. An old black-and-white print, gone brown at the edges. A hillside, steep and stony. A row of small dark openings in the rock, burial caves. And in front of them, coming up the slope, a bulldozer, its blade raised, and behind it a second one, and in the foreground, on the bare earth, things that Alan took for white stones until he looked again and saw that they were not stones.

At the bottom of the frame, in the corner of the print, someone had written in ink: *Tasarte. Agosto 1966.*

And beside the photograph, on the long map of the island, someone had taken a red pen and drawn circles. Not around Artenara, not around the cave. Around the south coast. Around Maspalomas, and Playa del Inglés, and San Agustín. Around every one of the great white resorts that lined the southern beaches like teeth, with their swimming pools and their golf courses and their two hundred thousand tourists a year.

Five circles. Six. And beside two of them, small and neat, the words *Montesdeoca H.S.A.*

There it was. Alan felt the plan snap back into place with a click. Montesdeoca's resorts, circled in red, on the wall of the man whose cave was being emptied. He didn't know what it meant. He didn't know how it connected to a fisherman drowned in his bed. But it connected. It had to.

He was reaching up to take the map off its pins when the light came on.

---

Rayco Baute was standing in the doorway.

Behind him, in the corridor, stood the guard in the grey uniform, red in the face. And behind the guard, because the noise in the hall had settled and somebody had seen the side door open and somebody else had asked where the Portuguese doctor's friend had gone, were Ignacio Montesdeoca, and the woman in pearls, and two of the professors, and Gail, with her hand over her mouth.

And at the back, behind all of them, with his notes still in his hand, Vasco.

Nobody said anything.

Alan stood behind Baute's desk in his cream linen suit, with the Montesdeoca file spread open under the lamp and his hand raised to the map on the wall, and he understood, in one long, cold, perfectly clear moment, exactly what he looked like.

Baute looked at the file. He looked at the map. He looked at the photograph of Tasarte in 1966, for a long moment, and something passed over his face and was gone.

Then he smiled.

It was the kindest smile Alan had ever seen, and it was the worst thing that had happened to him in eight months.

"Señor Sanches," he said gently. "You've found my office. I'm so sorry, I should have given you a tour. The rest of the museum is much more interesting." He came forward, and closed the file, quite calmly, and tucked it under his arm. "Shall we go back down? I believe the dessert is mango."

He turned to the people in the corridor.

"A misunderstanding," he said. "Señor Sanches is a bookseller. Booksellers are curious about paper. It's a professional illness. Please. Go back to your tables. Ignacio, I'm so sorry. You must think we're all mad."

Montesdeoca laughed, a big uncertain laugh, and the woman in pearls laughed too, high and nervous, and they began to drift away down the corridor, murmuring, looking back over their shoulders.

Vasco did not move.

He was standing in the corridor with his notes in his hand, and he was looking at Alan, and his face had no expression on it at all. It was the face of a man in a church who has just heard something terrible in confession and has been trained, for many years, not to let anything show.

Then he turned and went back down the stairs.

---

The dessert was mango.

Alan ate it. He didn't know what else to do. He sat at table seven between the German professor and the empty chair of the woman in pearls, who had moved to another table, and he ate his mango, and around him the dinner went on in a low, bright, unnatural murmur, the way a party goes on after someone has fallen over.

Vasco finished his talk. He finished it well. He came back to the lectern and apologised for the interruption with a small joke that got a small laugh, and went on to the last ten slides, and his voice did not shake once. When he finished there was long, warm applause, and Baute stood up to shake his hand, and held it a moment longer than he needed to, and said something quietly in his ear.

Vasco nodded.

He did not look at table seven. Not once.

At eleven o'clock, as the waiters began to clear and the quartet put away their instruments, Alan saw Carmen Trujillo standing by the door at the back of the hall. She had not been at the dinner. She was in her rust-coloured shirt and her desert boots, with her sunglasses on her head and a cigarette behind her ear, and she was looking at him across the room.

She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. She lowered her sunglasses, slowly, onto the bridge of her nose, and looked at him over the top of them.

Then she pushed them back up and went out into the night.

---

They took a taxi back to the hotel and neither of them said anything in the taxi.

They walked past Nieves in the lobby, and Nieves looked at their faces, and for the first time since Alan had arrived, she didn't raise her eyebrow. She looked down at her register and turned a page.

They went up in the lift, which sneezed at every floor, and neither of them laughed.

In the room, Vasco took off his good jacket and hung it on the back of the chair, carefully, squaring the shoulders. He took off his watch and put it on the bedside table. He went into the bathroom and Alan heard the tap run, and stop.

When he came out he lay down on his side of the bed, on top of the sheet, with his back to Alan, and turned off his lamp.

Alan sat on the edge of the bed in the dark.

"Vasco," he said.

"Not tonight."

"I can explain."

"I know you can." Vasco's voice was very quiet and very even. "You can always explain. That's what frightens me. Not tonight, Alan. Please. I've got to be up at five."

Alan sat there for a long time.

Through the open balcony doors came the sound of the sea, and under it, very faint, so faint he might have imagined it, the slow hiss and draw of waves on a beach that was not this beach, in a place that was not this place, a long way down in the dark.

He did not close the balcony doors. He lay down on his own side of the bed, on top of the sheet, with his back to Vasco's back, and the two of them lay there, not touching, a hand's breadth apart, awake, in the dark, until the grey light came.

## Chapter Four: The Fight

*Friday, the twenty-first of June. The summer solstice.*

Neither of them slept, and at five o'clock, when the alarm went, neither of them pretended to.

Vasco sat up and put his feet on the floor and sat there on the edge of the bed, with his back to Alan, with his head in his hands. Outside it was still dark. The ceiling fan went round. Down the corridor the lift sneezed, once, for nobody.

"The team's picking me up at half past," said Vasco. "Rayco wants everyone on site for the solstice. We're staying up there tonight. Tomorrow too, probably. Through to Monday."

"Through to Monday," said Alan.

"The sunrise on Monday is the one that matters. San Juan. I told you."

"You told me."

Vasco didn't move. Alan lay on his back and looked at the ceiling fan, going round.

"Are you going to ask me?" he said. "What I was doing in his office?"

"I know what you were doing in his office." Vasco's voice was muffled by his hands. "You were looking for something to prove that someone was doing something wrong. Because you'd decided that something was wrong, and instead of telling me, you went and broke into my director's office in the middle of my presentation, in front of the people who are paying for the work I've wanted to do my whole life."

"It's Montesdeoca," said Alan. "Vasco, listen to me. The discs from the cave are going into his van. One a day. He wants to build a hotel at Artenara, Chano says —"

"Chano." Vasco lifted his head. "The man at the kiosk."

"He knows everything."

"He sells lottery tickets, Alan." Vasco turned round on the bed, finally, and looked at him. His eyes were red. "The discs are going in Ignacio's van because Ignacio paid for the new display cases and offered the van to take them down. That's all. I signed the transfer sheets myself. They're in the conservation lab in the basement of the museum. You could have asked me. You could have just asked me."

Alan sat up.

"There's something else," he said.

"Of course there is."

"On Tuesday. On the promenade. There was a mist."

And he told him.

He told him all of it, there in the grey light, in the wrong order, too fast, the way you tell something you've been carrying for too long. The black mist coming in against the wind. The smell of the cistern. The helmet in Doña Pino's net, and the old fisherman drowned in his bed in La Isleta, and the list of names in her missal with the line through them. The tide on the steps at noon. The bell in Santa Ana with his family's mark on it, and the sealed letter, and the fax to Inês.

Vasco listened without moving. When it was done, he was quiet for a long time.

"Tuesday," he said at last.

"Vasco —"

"You saw it on Tuesday." His voice was very quiet. "You stood next to me on the promenade, and you saw it, and you smelled the thing that nearly killed you in that cistern, and you said *sunburn*." He laughed, a short, terrible laugh with no air in it. "Sunburn. And then you went to bed with me."

"I didn't want to spoil it for you."

"Spoil it."

"Your week. Your cave. You were so happy, Vasco. You were lit up. I've never seen you like that. I didn't want to walk in with my ghosts and turn it into a week about me."

"So instead you made it about you in front of eighty people."

Alan opened his mouth, and closed it.

Vasco stood up. He went to the window and stood with his back to the room, looking out at the dark sea. He was in his shorts, with his shoulders hunched, and he looked, suddenly, much older than thirty-nine, and much younger.

"Do you know what I've been doing for eight months?" he said to the window. "I've been waiting. Every morning. I make the coffee and I put it by the bed, and I wait for you to decide whether you're staying. Every single morning. I can see you deciding. You wake up and you look at the coffee, and you look at me, and I can see you thinking: is this the day I go?"

"That's not true."

"It's true. You know it's true." He turned round. "And I've never said anything. Because I thought, he'll stop. He'll get used to it. One morning he'll wake up and just drink the coffee. And every time you do something like this, every time you go off on your own and keep something from me and handle it, the way you always handle things, on your own, so you never have to need anybody, I think, that's him packing. That's him getting ready to leave."

"I'm not leaving."

"You don't know that. You've never known that."

The room was getting lighter. Outside, below the window, a truck went by on the promenade with a load of wooden pallets for the bonfires.

Vasco sat down on the chair by the window, heavily, as if his legs had stopped holding him up.

"I'm going to tell you something," he said. "I should have told you in January. I should have told you before I moved in. I didn't, because I was ashamed."

Alan waited.

"When I was in Rome," said Vasco, "at the Gregorian, studying, I was twenty-six. There was a man called Lorenzo. He was an architect. He was restoring a church in Trastevere, and I was writing about the same church, and we met on the scaffolding." He almost smiled. "He was very kind. He was very patient. He knew what I was, and he knew what I wanted to be, and he never once asked me to choose. He just waited. For four years. He'd say, whenever you're ready. Whenever you're ready, Vasco, I'll be here."

He looked down at his hands.

"And then one day I was ready. Or I nearly was. He'd found a flat for us. Two rooms, over a bakery, with a terrace where you could see the dome of Saint Peter's. He took me to see it. He'd already bought a coffee pot." His voice caught, and he waited until it didn't. "And I went home that night, and I packed my bags, and I took the night train to Lisbon. I didn't tell him. I didn't leave a note. I didn't call. I was so frightened of wanting something that much that I ran away from it in the middle of the night, like a thief. And I hid in the Church for nine years, because the Church was the one place he couldn't follow me."

The fan went round.

"I've been the one who leaves," said Vasco. "I know what it looks like from the inside. I know exactly what it looks like. I know what it does to the person who's left." He looked up at Alan, and his eyes were wet, and very steady. "And I won't be left by a man who's scared. Not again. Not by you. I'd rather go first."

Outside, a horn sounded. Twice.

Vasco stood up. He dressed quickly, in his field clothes, the dusty trousers and the shirt and the boots, and put his notebook in his bag, and his water bottle, and his hat. Alan sat on the bed and watched him and could not think of a single word.

At the door, Vasco stopped.

"If you're right," he said, without turning round. "About the mist. About the sea. If it's really here." A pause. "Then you should go home, Alan. Go back to Lisbon. Tomorrow, or Sunday. There's a flight on Sunday. Go and be safe."

"And you?"

"I'm needed here."

The door closed.

Alan sat on the bed and listened to Vasco's footsteps going down the corridor. He listened to the lift sneeze. He listened to the street door, and the Land Rover's door, and the engine, and the Land Rover pulling away along the promenade in the grey dawn, heading for the mountains.

On the bedside table, Vasco's coffee cup from yesterday morning was still standing where he'd left it, a ring of dried coffee in the bottom.

There was no coffee by the bed this morning.

---

He didn't go to La Isleta. He didn't go to the cathedral, or the phone box.

He went and sat on the sea wall.

It was the longest day of the year. He had read once that on the summer solstice the sun seems to stand still in the sky, which is where the word comes from, sol stitium, the sun standing, and he sat on the sea wall at the end of Las Canteras under the grey lid of the panza, with the sun somewhere above it, standing still, and he felt exactly the same. As if something had stopped. As if the world was holding its breath, and waiting to see which way it would turn.

The tide came up the steps. The fourth step this time. Nobody was filming it.

The men building the bonfire on the beach had nearly finished. It was three times the height of a man now, a great untidy pyramid of pallets and broken chairs and old doors and dried palm fronds, with a straw figure tied to the top of it, wearing a hat. Further along the beach there were others, a whole line of them, getting smaller into the distance, like the teeth of a comb.

He sat there for most of the day.

He thought about Lorenzo, whom he had never met, standing in an empty flat in Rome with a new coffee pot, waiting for someone who had already got on a train.

He thought about the coffee by the bed.

He thought about the fact that Vasco was right. That was the worst of it. Vasco was right about all of it. Every morning for eight months Alan had woken up and looked at the coffee and wondered, very quietly, at the back of his mind, whether this was the day. Not because he wanted to go. Because he was so frightened of how much he wanted to stay.

And Vasco had seen it. Every single morning. And made the coffee anyway.

At some point in the afternoon Chano came out of his kiosk and sat down on the wall beside him, with two bottles of beer, and handed him one, and said nothing at all for twenty minutes, which for Chano was a kind of miracle.

Then he said, "The doctor left early this morning."

"Yes."

"With a face."

"Yes."

Chano nodded slowly. He drank his beer and looked at the sea.

"My wife left me once," he said. "Nineteen seventy-nine. Three weeks. She went to her sister's in Telde."

"What did you do?"

"I went to Telde," said Chano. "What do you think?" He finished his beer and stood up. "She's still angry about it. Seventeen years. She says I took too long."

He went back to his kiosk.

In the afternoon, because he couldn't think of anywhere else to go, he went to the archive.

Carmen looked up as he came in. She had been at the dinner. She had seen everything. He waited for the sunglasses to come down.

They didn't. She got up, and went to a camping stove in the corner under the PROHIBIDO FUMAR sign, and made two small cups of coffee in a battered pot, and put one in front of him.

"That," she said, sitting back down, "was the stupidest thing I have ever seen anyone do in this building. And I once watched a deputy minister try on a mummy's necklace."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't a compliment." But something at the corner of her mouth had moved. "He left this morning. Your doctor. I heard."

"Everyone hears everything here."

"It's an island." She drank her coffee. "What do you want, Señor Sanches?"

"The cave," said Alan. "Tell me how it works. Not the story. The stone."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she opened a drawer and took out an envelope of photographs and spread them on the desk between them. Cave 6. The round gold dome. The triangles, hundreds of them, cut into the walls. The slot in the roof. A blade of light lying on the stone.

"From the spring equinox to the autumn one," she said, "the sun comes in through that channel every morning, and the light walks across the wall. Through the triangles. Touching them one after another, the whole summer long. At the solstice it reaches the biggest one, at the back." She touched it in the photograph, lightly, with one nicotine-stained finger. "They all point down. Every one. The textbooks say they're fertility symbols."

"And you?"

"A friend of mine thought they were arrows." She said it flatly, to the photograph. "She thought the discs in the channel were mirrors. Steering the light, to keep it on its path. She thought the whole thing was a sentence, and the light was reading it out loud, every day, so it stayed true."

"Your friend?"

"She's dead." Carmen gathered the photographs up again, quickly, and put them back in the envelope. "Las Palmas was founded on Saint John's day. The twenty-fourth of June. Did you know that? In the old reckoning, that was the solstice. The sun stands still for three days, and on the fourth morning it turns back. The Canarios knew it. The Church knew it. Rejón knew it when he planted his flag." She shut the drawer. "Everybody on this island has always known that morning matters. Nobody remembers why."

She looked at him then. Properly. And Alan saw, for the first time, that she was frightened.

"Go home, Señor Sanches," she said quietly. "Before Sunday."

---

Back on the sea wall, late in the afternoon, Alan thought about Artenara, and the mountain, and the long white road going up through the cloud. He could hire a car. He could be there in two hours.

And say what?

*I'm sorry. I'm scared. I'm scared of the sea and I'm scared of you, and I'm much more scared of you.*

He didn't hire a car.

---

The sun went down at nine. He couldn't see it go, under the panza, but he felt it: the grey light going dull, and then bruised, and then orange from the streetlights coming on along the promenade.

And then, out on the water, past the reef, the black line.

It came in against the wind, as it had on Tuesday, low and oily and slow. But it came in faster tonight, and thicker, and when it reached the beach it didn't stop at the waterline. It came up over the sand, between the unlit bonfires, curling round their bases like smoke round the legs of a table, and up over the first step, and the second, towards the promenade.

People were noticing now. A couple walking a dog stopped, and the dog barked, and pulled away. A group of teenagers on the wall stood up and laughed nervously and moved further along. A waiter came out of the café and looked at it, and crossed himself, and went back in, and Alan heard him say something to someone inside, and someone inside laugh.

"Panza," said a woman going past, to her husband, in the voice of someone who has lived through forty Junes. "It's just the panza, coming down low."

It was not the panza. The panza was above them, grey and high and still, where it always was. This was underneath it.

The smell came up the steps.

Alan stood up. He didn't decide to. He found he was standing, and then he found that he was walking, fast, away from the beach, away from the sea, inland, into the city, into the narrow streets where the mist couldn't follow, with his hands in his pockets and his head down, not running, not quite.

He walked for a long time.

He walked through Triana, past the shuttered shops and the people eating late at pavement tables, and over the dry riverbed of the Guiniguada on the big road bridge, and into Vegueta, into the old town, into the maze of cobbled streets around the cathedral, where the houses leaned together over his head and the iron lamps made yellow pools on the stones, and every corner had a candle burning in a little shrine.

He didn't know where he was going. That was the point. He didn't want to know.

It was the solstice, the night the sun stands still, and Alan Sanches was thirty-eight years old, and alone, and frightened, and he wanted, very badly, for one night, to be someone who had nothing to lose.

At the bottom of a narrow street behind the cathedral, there was a bar with its doors open, and music coming out of it, and warm yellow light, and laughter.

He went in.

## Chapter Five: Rafa

*Friday night.*

The bar had no name over the door. Later, he would never be able to remember what it was called, and he would understand that it didn't have one. It was just the bar, at the bottom of the street behind the cathedral, the one the locals went to.

Inside, it was one long narrow room with a low ceiling of dark beams, a zinc counter running down one side, and a few small tables against the other, crowded together. The walls were covered in paintings. Not good paintings, or not all of them: portraits, mostly, quick and loose, in charcoal and ink and watercolour, pinned up with drawing pins, overlapping, hundreds of them, so that every inch of the wall was faces. Old men with cigarettes. Women laughing. Fishermen. Children. A dog in a hat.

At the far end, two old men were slapping dominoes down on a table as if the dominoes had insulted them. A radio behind the bar was playing a song in Spanish about a woman who had gone to Venezuela and never come back. It smelled of rum and coffee and the sea.

And there was no mist in here at all.

Alan sat down at the counter.

The barman was at the other end, drying a glass, talking to someone. He was young, early twenties, lean and brown, with dark curly hair that was slightly too long and pushed back off his face as if he'd forgotten about it. He was wearing an open shirt in a bright cobalt blue, with the sleeves rolled up, over a white vest, and a thin silver chain at his throat, and there was a pencil tucked behind his ear and a bar towel over his shoulder. His fingers were smudged black, the fingertips and the sides of the hand, as if he'd been rubbing out shadows.

He saw Alan, and finished his sentence, and came down the bar, smiling. He had a wide, easy smile, the kind that doesn't ask anything.

"You look," he said, in Spanish, "like a man who needs rum."

"Is that how I look?"

"That," said the barman, "or a priest. You have the face of a man who's been listening to confessions all day." He was already pouring. "Rum. Local. It's terrible. You'll love it."

It was terrible. It was sweet and rough and tasted of burnt sugar and wood, and it went down Alan's throat like a lit match, and he loved it.

"Another," he said.

The barman laughed, and poured another, and leaned on the counter.

"Rafa," he said.

"Alan."

"Alan." Rafa tried it out. "English?"

"Portuguese. With an American mother. It's complicated."

"Everything's complicated," said Rafa. "Drink your rum."

---

He meant to have two. Then he would go back to the hotel, and sleep, and in the morning he would hire a car and drive up the mountain and say something to Vasco. He didn't know what. He would think of something on the way up.

He had three.

Rafa kept coming back. Between customers, between orders, between the domino players' arguments, he drifted back down the counter to where Alan was sitting, and leaned on his elbows, and talked. He talked about the paintings. They were his. He painted everyone who came in, he said, it was a sort of tax. He'd been doing it since he was sixteen. He wanted to go to art school in Madrid, and one day he would, when he'd saved enough, but he'd been saying that for seven years and he was beginning to suspect that he liked it here too much.

"The light," he said. "You can't get light like this in Madrid. In Madrid the light is like a government building. Here it's like —" He searched for the word, and gave up, and made a shape with his blackened fingers in the air. "Here it's alive."

"It's been grey since I arrived."

"That's the panza. That's not the light. The light's above it." Rafa grinned. "You have to go up to see it. Have you been up?"

"No," said Alan. "Not yet."

While they talked, Rafa had taken the pencil from behind his ear and a paper napkin from the stack on the counter, and he was drawing. He didn't look down at it. He looked at Alan, and talked, and laughed, and the pencil moved on the napkin by itself, quickly, lightly, like something with its own mind.

"What are you doing?"

"Paying my tax." Rafa turned the napkin round.

It was Alan. Three quarters, chin on hand, at the counter. A dozen lines, no more. And somehow it was exactly him. Not the face he saw in the mirror in the mornings, examining for grey. The face he had right now, tonight, here: tired and amused and somewhere behind the eyes, very far back, frightened.

"That's not fair," said Alan, quietly.

"Drawing's never fair," said Rafa. "That's what it's for."

---

At the far end of the counter, a young man was drinking alone.

Alan had noticed him when he came in, without noticing him, the way you notice furniture. He was about Rafa's age, slim, with short dark hair, and a T-shirt grey with dust, and he was drinking beer quickly, one after another, with a kind of grim determination, as if he were trying to put out a fire. He had been talking to Rafa when Alan came in. Now, as the bar filled up, he had begun to talk to anyone.

He was talking to the domino players, who weren't listening. He was talking to the radio. And sometimes, when Rafa went down to that end to serve him, he talked to Rafa, low and fast, leaning across the counter, and Rafa listened with his head on one side and his pencil moving on another napkin.

Alan heard bits of it. He wasn't listening. He was watching Rafa's hands.

"— every night, I'm telling you, every night this week, after the others are asleep —"

"— conservation, he says, conservation, but they never go in the van, Rafa, never, I carry them myself —"

"— down to the vault, the old granary, under the caves, and he locks it, and only he has the key —"

"— and the doctor doesn't know. The new one. The Portuguese. He thinks they're going to Las Palmas. He keeps asking me about the lab, and I keep saying, yes, doctor, the lab, and I can't look at him —"

"— if I say anything, I lose my place, I lose everything, he's been like a father —"

Someone at the tables called for Rafa. He came back up the bar, past Alan, and touched Alan's shoulder as he went, very lightly, and Alan felt it all the way down his arm, and didn't hear anything else at all.

---

The first time he tried to leave was at eleven.

He stood up, and put money on the counter, and said, "I should go."

"Already?" Rafa looked genuinely sorry. "You've only had three. Nobody leaves after three. It's bad luck. It's like leaving a story in the middle."

"I have to be up early."

"Why?"

And Alan stood there with his money on the counter, and couldn't think of a single reason. Nobody needed him to be up early. Nobody was waiting for him. There was no coffee by the bed.

"One more," he said, and sat back down.

---

The second time was at midnight.

He had asked to use the telephone. There was one on the wall by the door, an old black one with a coin box, and he'd fed it most of his change and called the museum, which was closed, and then called the night number for the museum, which was a security guard who had obviously been asleep, and asked him, in his best and most sober Spanish, whether there was any way to reach the dig at Risco Caído. The camp had a radio, he said. Could the museum radio the camp?

The guard went away for a long time. When he came back, he said that the museum had tried, and there was no answer from the camp. The camp radio must be switched off. Perhaps they were all asleep. It was midnight. Was it an emergency?

Alan stood by the phone with the receiver in his hand.

Was it an emergency?

"No," he said. "No. It can wait."

He hung up. He went back to the counter.

"He's not even answering his radio," he said, to nobody, and Rafa, who had been watching him from the other end of the bar, poured him another rum without being asked, and didn't say anything, and that was somehow worse and better than anything he could have said.

---

The young man at the end of the bar left at about one. He went past Alan on his way to the door, unsteady, and stopped for a moment, and looked at him, as if he were trying to remember where he'd seen him before. He had a nice face, Alan thought, vaguely. An unhappy face.

Then he went out into the street, and was gone.

Rafa came and leaned on the counter opposite Alan and looked after him.

"Yeray," he said. "He's on the dig, up at Artenara. He comes down when he can't stand it. He's got a lot on his mind, poor bastard."

Alan should have asked. Later, he would think about this moment more than any of the others. He should have said: *The dig at Artenara? The dig at Risco Caído? What's on his mind?*

"Hasn't everyone," said Alan, and drank his rum.

Rafa looked at him for a moment. Then he picked up the napkin he'd been drawing on while Yeray talked, and turned it over, and drew something on the back, and put it in his pocket.

---

The third time was at two, when the domino players had gone, and the radio was off, and Rafa was putting the chairs up on the tables.

Alan stood up. He put money on the counter.

"I should go," he said.

Rafa put the last chair up. He came round the end of the bar, wiping his hands on the towel, and stood in front of Alan, very close. He was a little shorter than Alan, and he had to tilt his head back slightly, and he did, and looked at him, and smiled, the wide, easy smile.

"I'm just talking," said Alan. "I'm just… I just needed somewhere to sit. I'm not —"

"I know," said Rafa. "Me too."

They stood there in the empty bar under the hundreds of painted faces.

"My studio's upstairs," said Rafa. "I've got coffee. Real coffee. Not the terrible rum."

Alan didn't move.

And then Rafa asked it. He asked it simply, lightly, the way you'd ask someone the time, not looking away. He had obviously asked it before. He was the kind of man who asked.

"Is there someone?"

The bar was very quiet.

Alan thought of a man asleep in a hotel room with one arm thrown across his chest. He thought of the coffee by the bed. He thought of the two small conditions, spoken quietly in a kitchen in Lisbon in January, as if they were nothing. *Tell me first. Not after. And never to settle a fight.*

He thought: *He told me to go home.*

He thought: *He's not even answering his radio.*

He thought: *I'm thirty-eight years old, and I have never once in my life stayed anywhere long enough to be left, and I'm not going to start tonight.*

"No," he said.

And then, because some part of him still wanted to be caught, still wanted Rafa to hear it, he said it again, quieter, with a pause in front of it that anyone could have heard.

"…No."

Rafa looked at him for a long moment, with his head a little on one side, the way he had looked at Yeray.

Then he reached up and took the pencil from behind his ear, and put it in his pocket, and took Alan's hand in his blackened fingers, and led him to the stairs.

---

The studio was one room under the roof, hot from the day, with a big window open onto the dark rooftops of Vegueta and the two towers of the cathedral black against the orange sky. It smelled of turpentine and coffee and the sea. There were canvases stacked against every wall, and sketchbooks on every surface, and a mattress on the floor under the window with a white sheet on it, and the white sheet glowed in the light from the street.

Rafa didn't make the coffee.

And Alan, who had been so careful all week, so careful of Vasco's happiness, so careful of his sunburn, so careful of the words he didn't say, wasn't careful at all.

It was nothing like the hotel. That was what he would remember. Nothing was familiar. Nothing was easy, or known. Rafa was young and quick and laughing and completely unafraid, of Alan, of himself, of anything, and for an hour, in the hot dark under the roof, with the bells of Santa Ana striking the quarters a hundred metres away, Alan was twenty-three again, and nobody knew his name, and nobody would notice if he was late, and there was nothing he could lose.

It was wonderful. That was the worst of it. He had hoped, somewhere, that it would be terrible. It wasn't. It was warm and real and generous and kind, and Rafa laughed into his shoulder, and said something in Spanish that Alan didn't catch, and Alan laughed too, and for an hour he didn't think about anything at all.

Afterwards, Rafa fell asleep almost at once, the way young men do, sprawled across the mattress like a cat in the sun.

Alan lay on his back and looked at the ceiling.

Through the open window, the bells of Santa Ana struck three. His family's bell, among them. *Sanches made me.*

He lay there and listened to it ring, and the sound went on in the dark long after the striking stopped, the way a bell does, humming, remembering, and it seemed to him that it was asking him something, over and over, in a language he had always understood and always pretended not to.

He didn't answer it.

---

On the other side of the city, on the third floor of a block of flats in Guanarteme, two streets back from Las Canteras, a retired lifeguard called Agustín Ramos was asleep in his armchair in front of the television.

He was seventy-one. He had been a lifeguard on Las Canteras for forty years, and in forty years he had pulled more than two hundred people out of the sea, tourists and children and drunks and fishermen, and he had never lost one. There was a plaque about it at the town hall.

What the plaque didn't say was that he had become a lifeguard because, when he was thirty-three, a rip current at the north end of the beach had taken him out past the reef on a calm afternoon in August 1958 and held him under, and turned him over, and filled his mouth and his nose and his lungs, and then, just when he had stopped fighting, an old man in a rowing boat had got an oar to him, and then a hand, and hauled him over the side like a fish onto a slab.

He had spent the rest of his life paying the sea back, one swimmer at a time.

Tonight the television was showing a late film, a western, with the sound turned down low. The window was closed. The flat was dry, and warm, and smelled of coffee and an old man's cigarettes.

At three o'clock, the picture on the television dissolved into grey static, and the static began to make a sound.

Agustín Ramos woke up. He knew the sound before he was awake. He had listened to it every day for forty years. It was the sound of waves at the north end of Las Canteras, on a calm afternoon, past the reef.

The carpet was wet around his slippers. Then his ankles were wet.

He did not try to get up. He was seventy-one, and he was very tired, and he had always known, if he was honest, that this was a loan, not a gift.

"Thirty-eight years," he said to the sea, quite calmly. "You were patient. I'll give you that."

In the morning, his neighbour found him in his armchair, with the television hissing, and the window closed, and his lungs full of the sea.

## Chapter Six: The Morning After

*Saturday, the twenty-second of June.*

He woke up because the bells of Santa Ana were ringing for eight o'clock, and for a few seconds, with his eyes still closed, he didn't know where he was, and he was happy.

Then he opened his eyes.

The studio was full of grey morning light. The window was still open. Rafa was asleep on his front beside him with one arm hanging off the edge of the mattress, his face turned away, breathing slowly. His back was brown and smooth and young, and there was charcoal on his shoulder blade in the shape of a thumbprint.

And propped against the wall at the foot of the mattress, where Alan would see it the moment he woke, was a sheet of heavy paper with a drawing on it.

It was Alan, asleep.

Rafa must have done it at dawn, while he slept. It was charcoal, quick and soft, a few dozen lines and some smudges of shadow. Alan on his side, one hand under his cheek, the sheet down around his waist. His mouth was a little open. His face was completely unguarded, younger than it had any right to be, peaceful in a way he had not seen it in a mirror for years.

It was the most beautiful drawing anyone had ever made of him, and he looked at it, and he felt sick.

He got up very quietly and found his clothes, scattered across the floor in a way that told the whole story without needing anyone to tell it, and put them on. His cream linen suit. It smelled of rum and turpentine and someone else's skin.

He should go now. Before Rafa woke. That was what he had always done. Leave the money on the counter and go, and be someone else by lunchtime.

He didn't go.

He went to the window and stood there with his hands on the sill, looking at the two black towers of the cathedral, and the grey sky, and he made himself think it, in words, all the way to the end.

*I lied to him. He asked me, straight out, and I lied. And Vasco said two things. Only two. Tell me first. Never to settle a fight. And I broke both of them in one night, and the second one I broke on purpose.*

He stood there for a long time.

On the table under the window, among the coffee cups and the brushes in jars, there was a sketchbook lying open, and tucked into it, like a bookmark, was a paper napkin from the bar.

He looked at it without meaning to. It was a drawing of the young man from the end of the counter, the unhappy one, Yeray: his face side-on, leaning in, mid-sentence, his hand around a bottle. And around the drawing, in the margins, in Rafa's quick, sloping pencil, were words. Phrases. Things Yeray had said, written down the way some people doodle while they listen on the phone, half-attending, catching the bits that stuck.

*Every night this week.*

*Conservation — but they never go in the van.*

*I carry them myself.*

*Down to the vault. Old granary under the caves.*

*Only Baute has the key.*

*The Portuguese doctor doesn't know.*

Alan read it three times.

Then he sat down, slowly, on the edge of the table, among the brushes, and put the napkin down very carefully, as if it might go off.

Not Montesdeoca. Not the van, not the hotel, not the circled resorts on the map. Not a greedy man with a cigar who wanted a theme park in a cave.

Baute.

The discs weren't going to the city. They were going down into a vault under the mountain, every night, one by one, and only Baute had the key, and Vasco didn't know. Vasco had signed the transfer sheets himself. Vasco was up there now, on the mountain, with Baute, through to Monday. Calling him Rayco.

And Alan had heard it. He had heard every word of it, last night, at the bar, and he had been too busy watching Rafa's hands to listen.

The clue had been in front of him the whole time, while he was busy lying.

---

"There's coffee," said Rafa from the mattress, sleepily. "Real coffee. I promised."

He made it on a gas ring in the corner, in an old aluminium pot, barefoot, in his shorts, yawning, with his hair standing up on one side. It was very good coffee. They drank it at the table by the window, with the sketchbook between them.

Rafa saw his face, and saw the napkin, and saw where Alan's eyes kept going.

"Yeray," he said. "Poor bastard. He talks when he drinks. I write it down. It's a bad habit. I'll stop one day." He looked at Alan over his cup. "You know him?"

"No," said Alan. "I know the man he works for."

And then, because there was nothing else left to do with the morning, and because he had spent the whole of the last week not saying things, he put his cup down and said the other thing.

"Rafa. Last night. When you asked me if there was someone."

Rafa didn't move.

"There is," said Alan. "His name's Vasco. We've been together eight months. He's up on the mountain at Artenara, on the dig. Yeray's dig. We had a fight yesterday morning, and he left, and I came out last night to be someone who didn't have anything to lose. And I lied to you. I'm sorry. You asked me straight, and you deserved a straight answer, and I didn't give you one."

Rafa looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked down at his coffee.

"I know," he said.

"You know?"

"You said it twice." Rafa shrugged one brown shoulder. "Nobody says no twice unless it's yes." He smiled, a little, but it was a different smile from last night's. Older. "I'm twenty-three, Alan. I'm not stupid. I just thought, he's sad, and he's beautiful, and he's leaving on Sunday anyway. What does it matter?" He turned the cup in his blackened fingers. "It matters. I know it matters. I'm sorry too."

They sat there in the grey light.

Then Rafa got up, and went to the foot of the mattress, and picked up the charcoal drawing of Alan asleep. He looked at it for a moment. He blew on it, gently, to lift off the loose dust. And he held it out.

"Take it," he said.

"I can't."

"Take it. It's yours. It's not mine. I just saw it." He put it into Alan's hands. "It's the last time you'll look that young. You should have something to remember it by."

---

He walked back to the hotel through the Saturday morning streets with the drawing rolled up under his arm, and the napkin in his pocket.

The city was getting ready for the fiesta. There were flags going up across the streets, and strings of coloured bulbs, and on every corner men were unloading crates of beer from trucks. In Triana a brass band was practising in a doorway, badly. Everywhere he looked there were posters: *FIESTAS FUNDACIONALES. SAN JUAN 1996. 518 AÑOS.* Five hundred and eighteen years since the city was founded, on the twenty-fourth of June, on the morning the sun turns back.

The beach, when he got to it, was full of people. Families had come down early to claim their places for Sunday night, laying out towels and coolers and folding chairs around the bonfires. The tide was on the fourth step. Nobody seemed to notice.

Nieves was behind the desk.

She looked at him as he came in. At the suit, and the hour, and the roll of paper under his arm, and his face. Her eyebrow didn't move at all. That was how he knew how bad he looked.

"Any messages?" he said.

"No."

"From Dr. Teles? From the dig?"

Nieves looked down at the pigeonhole for his room. It was empty. She looked at it for slightly longer than she needed to, as if she were hoping something might appear in it, for his sake.

"No," she said. "Nothing, señor." And then, after a pause, in a lower voice: "There's coffee in the machine. It's still terrible. I'll bring you some."

---

The room was exactly as he had left it, and exactly as Vasco had left it the morning before. The bed unmade on one side. Vasco's good jacket on the back of the chair, shoulders squared. Vasco's watch on the bedside table, forgotten.

And on the tiles by Vasco's side of the bed, a footprint.

A wet footprint. A man's bare foot, long and narrow, dark on the terracotta. Just one. Pointing at the bed. As if someone had stepped out of the sea, and stood there in the night, looking down at where Alan should have been sleeping.

Alan stood in the doorway and looked at it. The balcony doors were shut. The bathroom was dry.

He thought, for no reason he could name, of his father. Pedro Sanches, who had gone out fishing off Cascais one morning in 1962, when Alan was four, and had never come back, and whose face Alan could no longer remember except as a shape against a bright window.

He knelt down and touched the footprint. It was cold, and his fingertips came away tasting of salt.

By the time Nieves knocked with the coffee, it had dried, and there was nothing there at all.

---

He called Inês from the phone box at eleven, with the last of his change.

"Where have you been?" she said, before he could speak. "I've been calling the hotel since eight o'clock. The woman at the desk says you didn't come back last night. The woman at the desk sounds like a disappointed nun."

"Inês."

"Alan. Where were you?"

He stood in the phone box with his forehead against the glass. Outside, Chano was serving a queue of people buying fireworks.

"I did something," he said. "Last night. Vasco and I had a fight. He went up the mountain. And I went out, and I met someone, and I slept with him."

There was a long silence on the line from Lisbon.

"And you're telling me," said Inês. "Before you've told Vasco."

"Yes."

"Alan, the rules. The two rules. He told me about them, in the shop, in January, he was so proud of himself for being modern. Tell me first. Never to settle a fight."

"I know."

"So you broke both of them."

"I know, Inês."

She was quiet again. When she spoke, her voice was different. Not soft. She didn't do soft. But steady.

"Tell him," she said. "Today. Not when it's convenient. Not when it's safe. Not when you've worked out how to make it sound better. Today."

"I can't reach him. He's on the mountain. The radio's off."

"Then find a way," said Inês. "You've always been very good at finding ways into places you shouldn't be. Find a way into one you should."

He closed his eyes.

"And Alan," said Inês. "The fax. The letter from the cathedral. I've read it."

He opened them again.

"It took me two days and a dictionary of eighteenth-century abbreviations from the national library and a professor of palaeography who thinks I'm in love with him," said Inês. "It's from the founders. Three of them signed it. Your family. Bartolomeu Sanches is the first signature." Paper rustled on the line. "Are you sitting down?"

"I'm in a phone box."

"You know about the voyage. Now listen to what they found. A lock. That's the word they use, *fechadura*. A lock the old priestesses of the island made, the native women, in fourteen eighty-three, to hold the Sea. With a capital S. The same Sea, Alan. The one from the cistern. They say the priestesses called it, to drown the Castilian ships, and it came, and it didn't stop. It took the ships and then the coast and then their own villages. And the priestesses shut it out again, with the sun. With light. In a cave."

Alan's hand was tight on the receiver.

"And there's a part about how the priestesses shut it," Inês went on. "I'll read it to you. I've translated it. 'To shut the door again, one must stand in its water, at the door, at the first sunrise of the turning, and set the stones while it rises. The last of the priestesses did this. She set the stones, and the Sea withdrew. And it drew her down with it as it went, and she was not seen again.'"

She stopped.

"And then there's a line at the end," she said, "in a different hand. Underlined. 'Let any who come after us remember: if the door is opened in your time, the Sea will strike first at any who understand the lock, for it will not be shut again; and at our blood above all, for it knows the hands that bound it once. And it will come for all that was ever taken back from it: every soul it once had in its grip and lost. If it is opened in your time, and one must stand in the water, pray God it is not you.'"

The phone box was very quiet.

"Alan," said Inês. "The cistern. It backed away from you because of whose blood you are. In Lisbon that saved you. Here, with the door opening, it's the reason it'll want you first."

"I know."

"So if it's opened in your time —"

"I know, Inês."

The pips went. The money was running out.

"Tell him today," said Inês, very fast. "Tell him everything. And then come home. Promise me. Promise me you'll come h—"

The line went dead.

---

Chano had a newspaper for him, and a face.

"Another one," he said, and turned the paper round on the counter. A short column on page six. *Ex-socorrista hallado muerto en su domicilio.* Former lifeguard found dead at home. Agustín Ramos, seventy-one. Guanarteme. Natural causes.

"My cousin's husband knew him," said Chano. "Forty years on the beach. Two hundred people he pulled out. Two hundred. And he drowns in his armchair, in front of the television." He crossed himself. "They say heart. Heart, with seawater in his lungs. That's two this week. Pepe, and now Agustín."

"Did he ever nearly drown?" said Alan. "Agustín. When he was young?"

Chano stared at him.

"Fifty-eight," he said slowly. "In a rip. Everybody knows that story. It's why he became a lifeguard." He looked at Alan for a long moment. "How did you know?"

Alan didn't answer. He was looking at the beach. At the tide on the fourth step. At the families laying out their towels around the bonfires for tomorrow night.

---

He tried. Later, he would need to know that he had tried.

He went to the museum, to find Carmen, and the museum was closed for the weekend, with a sign on the door, and when he hammered on it the security guard from the night before came and looked at him through the glass and shook his head.

He asked the guard to radio the dig. The guard tried. No answer. He tried again. No answer. The camp radio was dead.

He went to the car-hire office in Triana, and every car on the island was booked for the San Juan weekend, and the woman behind the desk laughed at him, not unkindly. He went to the bus station, and the bus to Artenara went once a day, at seven in the morning, and had gone. He stood in the middle of the bus station with a timetable in his hand and thought about walking. It was fifty kilometres, uphill, through the cloud.

He would take the bus in the morning. Seven o'clock. He would be at the dig by ten. He would find Vasco, and tell him everything, and they would deal with Baute together. Tomorrow. First thing.

It was the most reasonable decision he had made all week, and he hated it.

---

The sea came into La Isleta at seven in the evening.

He heard about it from the street before he saw it, people running and shouting, and he followed them, across the neck of sand and into the narrow streets, and found them flooded.

Not from the harbour. Not from a wave. There had been no storm, no swell. The harbour was flat and grey and calm. But the water was coming up through the streets anyway, up through the drains and the cracks between the cobbles, quietly, steadily, black and cold, ankle deep, then shin deep, in the low streets around Calle Pescadores where Pepe Santana had lived. Old women were standing in their doorways with brooms, sweeping it back, and it came in around the brooms. Men were carrying televisions up stairs. A child was crying. A dog swam past, paddling furiously, with an expression of enormous offence.

And under the grey panza, low on the water, the black mist lay over the harbour like smoke over a battlefield.

Alan waded through it to Doña Pino's door.

The door was open. The water was over the step. Inside, in the dim front room, Don Lolo was sitting on a kitchen chair, with his trousers rolled to the knee and his feet in the black water, mending a net in his lap by the light of a candle, as calmly as if he were on the quay on a summer afternoon.

Behind him, through a doorway, on a narrow bed, lay Doña Pino.

She was soaked. Her hair was wet. Her black dress was wet. She was coughing, long, wrenching coughs that brought up water, salt water, onto the pillow. But she was alive, and sitting halfway up, propped on her elbows, glaring.

"I fell asleep," she said, between coughs, furious. "I never fall asleep in the afternoon. I sat down to say a rosary and I fell asleep, and I woke up and I was drowning in my own bed." She pointed a shaking finger at Lolo. "He doesn't sleep. Fifty-five years married, and he has never once slept through the night. I always said it would kill me. It saved me. He heard the sea in the room. He pulled me up by my hair."

"By your collar," said Don Lolo, without looking up from his net.

"By my hair!"

"Collar," said Don Lolo. He looked up at Alan, and took the cigarette out of the corner of his mouth, and looked at it, and put it back.

"Fifty-five years," he said, to Alan, in the first full sentence Alan had ever heard him speak. "And she still hasn't forgiven me for the first one."

---

Later, when the water had begun, slowly, impossibly, to sink back down into the drains it had come from, and the neighbours had come with towels and soup and opinions, and Doña Pino had been put in dry clothes and had refused to go to the hospital in terms that made a young doctor go red to the ears, Alan sat by her bed.

She had her missal on her knee. She opened it to the inside cover, and looked at the names. Two crossed out. Two not.

"Tomás," she said, "is in Venezuela. Maybe it can't reach Venezuela. Maybe it can. God knows." She looked at her own name for a long moment. "And me."

"It came back for you," said Alan. "And it'll come again."

"Yes." She looked at him. "You know a lot about what the sea does, Portuguese."

"It's coming for me too," said Alan. "Not for the same reason. But it's coming."

She went very still.

He told her. Not all of it. The cistern. The black water at his chest. The thing in the dark that had looked at him, and known his blood, and backed away. The bells. His family.

When he had finished, Doña Pino was quiet for a long time. Then she took a pen from the table beside the bed, an old fountain pen, and unscrewed it, and held it out to him, and held out the missal, open at the names.

He took them.

At the bottom of the list, under her name, in the same blue ink that had gone purple with time for everyone else, Alan wrote his own name. *Alan Sanches.* His hand was perfectly steady.

Doña Pino looked at it. Then she took the missal back, and closed it, and held it against her chest.

"Now God knows too," she said. "Go home, Portuguese. Go to bed. Say your prayers."

---

He went back to the hotel. He lay down on the bed in his clothes, on his side, on the side that was his, facing Vasco's side, and Vasco's watch on the bedside table, and the place on the tiles where the footprint had been.

He put the rolled-up drawing on the pillow beside him.

The phone on the bedside table did not ring.

He lay there and looked at it. He looked at it for a long time, in the dark, with the sound of the sea coming in through the balcony doors, and under it, closer every night, the other sound.

On the desk by the door was his plane ticket. Lisbon, via Madrid. Sunday, eleven fifteen.

Tomorrow there would be two things to choose between: a bus at seven that went up the mountain, and a plane at eleven fifteen that went home. One of them might save his life.

He didn't know which.

He lay awake, and stared at the phone, and it didn't ring.

## Chapter Seven: Vasco

*Saturday, the twenty-second of June. Risco Caído.*

Vasco Teles had not slept since Thursday, and at seven o'clock on Saturday morning he was standing on the edge of a cliff above the clouds, trying very hard not to think about Alan.

It was the most beautiful place he had ever stood.

The camp was on a narrow shelf of rock high on the side of a deep barranco below the village of Artenara, at almost twelve hundred metres. Behind it the cliff went up, red and gold and honeycombed with caves, dozens of them, small dark doorways cut into the soft rock by people who had lived and prayed here a thousand years ago. Below it, the cliff went down, and down, into pines and then into nothing.

Because at about a thousand metres, the world stopped, and the cloud began.

The panza de burro lay under him like a frozen sea. It was perfectly flat and perfectly white and it went all the way to the horizon in every direction, filling the valleys, swallowing the coast, so that the island was gone and there was nothing in the world but this ridge of red rock and pine, standing up out of a white ocean into a sky so blue it hurt. Far away to the west, across forty kilometres of white, the great volcano of Teide on Tenerife stood up out of the cloud like another island.

And over it all, the sun. Blazing, hot already, enormous, with nothing between it and him.

Down there, under the cloud, it was grey. Down there, Alan was asleep in a hotel room in the grey, or not asleep. Down there was the sea.

Alan would make a joke about this, thought Vasco. Something about heaven, and being overdressed for it.

"I'm not thinking about Alan," he said aloud, to the cloud.

The cloud didn't answer.

---

The camp was a scatter of green canvas tents, a long trestle table under a tarpaulin covered in finds trays and labels and brushes, a petrol generator that coughed all night and was off now, and a folding table by the cliff edge where the radio sat in its metal case with its long aerial pointing at the sky.

Vasco went to the radio. He stood looking at it for a while.

He could call the museum. The museum could call the hotel. In ten minutes he could be hearing Alan's voice.

And say what?

The radio was switched off. He looked at the little dead dial, and felt something like relief, and was ashamed of the relief.

"Off," he said. "Good. I don't want to talk to him either."

He didn't touch it.

---

The expedition files were on the finds table, in a row of grey box files, and he went through them because he needed something to do with his hands.

He had been through them before, in his first week, looking for the measurements of the light channel. But this time he went to the back of the last box, where the papers of his predecessor had been filed and forgotten.

Dr. Elena Suárez. The sacred-light specialist before him. Rayco had mentioned her once, on the first day, with a strange flat look on his face, and said she had died in an accident in December, on the mountain road, and changed the subject. Vasco had not liked to ask.

Her drawings were beautiful.

They were sun-path charts of Cave 6. Dozens of them, on squared paper, in a fine black pen. The wall of the cave drawn in elevation, every engraved triangle numbered, and over it, in red, the track of the beam of light from the roof channel, for every hour of the day, on every important date of the year. The equinoxes. The solstices. The first of May. All Saints. A whole year of light, plotted and timed and checked and checked again, in small, upright, patient handwriting.

"Suárez," said Vasco, turning the pages. "My predecessor."

On the last chart, for the summer solstice, she had drawn the beam's track in red as usual. And then, beside it, in pencil, she had drawn a second track. It went somewhere else. It didn't touch the triangles at all. And beside it, in the margin, in handwriting that was no longer small or upright or patient, she had written one word and underlined it three times.

*Puerta.* Door.

Vasco looked at it for a long time.

"Good hand," he said quietly. "Better than mine."

He put the charts in his bag.

---

His own notebook was in the bag too. He took it out and looked at it, because he couldn't help it.

Twelve pages of measurements from yesterday, the solstice. Clinometer readings. Compass bearings. The time the beam entered the channel, and the time it reached each numbered triangle, and the time it left.

And on the thirteenth page, one sentence, crossed out four times, so hard that the pen had gone through the paper.

If you held the page up to the light you could still read it.

*I'm sorry.*

It wasn't for the cave.

---

Yeray was at the camping stove, making coffee, and he looked terrible.

He was grey under his tan and his eyes were bloodshot and he was moving very carefully, the way young men move when they have drunk too much and are trying to keep their heads perfectly level.

"Morning, doctor," he said. "Coffee? It's terrible."

"Then yes," said Vasco.

Yeray poured it into a tin mug and handed it over without meeting his eyes. They stood side by side at the edge of the camp and looked at the cloud.

"I was in Vegueta last night," said Yeray, after a while. "At Rafa's bar. You know it? Behind the cathedral."

"No."

"You should go. Everyone goes." Yeray drank his coffee. "There was a Portuguese guy there."

Vasco didn't move.

"Funny," said Yeray. "Making Rafa laugh. Making everyone laugh." He frowned into his mug, as if trying to remember. "Sad, though. Underneath. You could see it. Like a man at his own party who doesn't know why he came."

There were a great many Portuguese men in the world. There were probably several in Las Palmas on any given night.

"Most Portuguese men are sad," said Vasco. "It's the national sport."

Yeray laughed, briefly, and winced at the noise. Then he was quiet again. Vasco could feel him gathering himself, the way a man gathers himself before jumping into cold water.

"Doctor," said Yeray. "The discs."

"Yes?"

"Dr. Baute says they're going down to Las Palmas. For conservation. To the lab."

"Yes," said Vasco. "I signed the sheets."

Yeray looked at him then, for the first time, straight in the face, and Vasco saw that the boy was frightened. Not hungover. Frightened.

"They're not going to Las Palmas," said Yeray.

The cloud lay below them, perfectly still.

"What do you mean?"

But Yeray had already looked away. He threw the rest of his coffee out over the cliff edge, in a brown arc that vanished into the white.

"Forget it," he said. "I'm hungover. I talk rubbish." He was already walking away towards the tents. Over his shoulder, not turning round, he said: "Ask Dr. Baute. Please. Not me."

Vasco stood at the edge of the cliff with his tin mug.

A Portuguese man in Rafa's bar. Funny. Sad underneath.

He told himself he was not thinking about Alan.

---

Cave 6 was a hundred metres along the cliff, reached by a narrow path cut into the rock, with a rope fixed to iron pins for a handrail.

You came to it round a buttress, and it was nothing. A small square doorway in the cliff, barely higher than a man, dark inside. You had to stoop to go in.

And then you straightened up, and you were inside a dome.

It was round, about six metres across, carved out of the soft volcanic rock by hand, smooth and pale and gold. The walls rose and curved inward to a peak high above his head, and every centimetre of them was covered in engravings. Triangles. Hundreds of triangles, cut into the stone, large and small, single and in rows and clusters, some deep and sharp, some worn almost to nothing.

Every one of them pointed down.

And high in the dome, just off the peak, was the channel. A narrow slot cut through the rock to the sky, angled, so that at this time of the morning a blade of sunlight came through it, thin and bright and white as a knife, and lay against the western wall, burning, on bare rock between the triangles.

Vasco stood in the middle of the cave and felt, as he had every morning for eight days, his heart turn over.

He had spent his life in churches built to receive the sun. Little Romanesque chapels in the Alentejo where on one morning a year the light came through the east window and fell exactly on the altar. Gothic cathedrals where it touched a particular saint's face at a particular hour on her feast day. He knew the feeling of standing inside a building that had been made to listen to the sky.

This was older than all of them. And better.

"The priestesses did it first," he said to the cave.

Then he looked at the channel's lip, where the discs were.

Along the edge of the slot, where the light came in, there were seven sockets cut into the rock. Small, round, shallow cups, in a row. In each of them, when he had first come to the cave a week ago, there had been a disc of obsidian: black volcanic glass, polished to a mirror, about the size of a man's palm, set at an angle in the socket. Or so he had thought. He realised now that he had never counted them on that first day. He had been looking at the light.

Five of the sockets were empty now. Only two discs were left.

He went up the wooden ladder that leaned against the wall below the channel, and looked at the empty sockets closely, with a torch.

The rock around them was scored. Fresh scratches, bright and pale in the old weathered stone, deep, parallel, the marks of a blade forced in under the edge of each disc and levered. Not the careful work of a conservator with a wooden spatula. Steel.

"Fresh tool marks," he said quietly. "Steel, not stone. Someone's been prying these out with a modern chisel."

He looked at one of the remaining discs. It was black, and perfect, and in it, in the shadow of the channel, he could see his own face: unshaven, hollow-eyed, a man in a hat who hadn't slept.

"I look like a man who hasn't slept," he told it.

---

He spent the morning on it.

He didn't decide to. It was what he did when he was unhappy; it was what he had always done. When Lorenzo was gone, when the Church was gone, when there was nothing left to hold on to, there was always a building, and a light, and a question.

He had Elena's charts. He had his clinometer and his compass and a stick of chalk. And he had time, because the others were all down on the lower terrace sieving soil, and Rayco had gone down the mountain at dawn to the village for supplies and would not be back until lunch.

The beam moved. That was the first thing. Over the next hours, as the sun climbed, the blade of light slid slowly across the western wall, a few centimetres every quarter of an hour. Vasco waited, and marked its position with chalk every half hour, a small cross and the time.

Then he laid Elena's solstice chart against the wall, and compared.

Her red line, the line the beam should follow on this date, ran through the triangles. It crossed the largest cluster on the western wall in the morning, and walked through a row of smaller ones, and reached the great triangle at the back of the cave, the biggest of them all, a metre high.

His chalk crosses didn't follow her line. They were off it. Five or six centimetres off at first, and then more, the gap widening as the hours went on. By eleven o'clock the beam was falling on bare stone, nowhere near a triangle, as if it were lost.

The beam was off its path.

Vasco stood back and looked at the chalk marks for a long time.

Then he climbed the ladder again, to the two discs that were left, and very carefully, with his fingertips, he turned one.

It moved in its socket, stiffly, with a faint gritty sound, stone on glass. He turned it a few degrees. And on the wall across the cave, the blade of light bent.

It jumped. It slid sideways twenty centimetres, all at once, and landed on a triangle, squarely, and lit it up, so that every cut line of it stood out in gold and black.

Vasco stood on the ladder with his hand on the disc and his heart going very fast.

The discs weren't decoration. They weren't offerings, or ornaments. They were mirrors, set at angles in the channel, to catch the light coming in and steer it. To hold the beam on its path across the triangles, day after day, year after year, exactly where the priestesses wanted it.

And with five of them gone, the light was falling wherever it liked.

---

He sat on the floor of the cave with Elena's charts spread around him and worked it out.

It took him two hours. He used her measurements of the channel and his own of the sun, and the positions of the empty sockets, and a great deal of trigonometry that he had not done since school and had to rebuild from first principles on the backs of her charts, in pencil, with the chalk dust on his fingers.

If all seven discs were in place, each at its proper angle, then on the morning of the twenty-fourth of June, the first sunrise when the sun, after three days of standing still, visibly began to turn back, the beam would come in through the channel and go from mirror to mirror down the length of the slot, and come out the other end and cross the cave, and land.

He drew the line on the chart.

It landed on the great triangle at the back of the cave. The biggest of them all. Dead centre.

The angle the last disc had to hold, the seventh, at the end of the channel, the one that sent the light across the cave for the last time, was very particular. He worked it out three times, to be sure.

When he had the number, he sat back against the wall and laughed. A short, startled laugh, alone in the cave, echoing up into the dome.

"Twenty-three degrees," he said. "Twenty-six minutes."

The angle of the Earth's tilt. The latitude of the Tropic of Cancer, where the sun stands still at the solstice before it turns. The number every schoolchild learns and forgets.

"The tilt of the world," said Vasco. "Of course it is."

And then, because he was thorough, and because Elena Suárez had drawn a second line on her last chart and written a word beside it, he worked out the other thing. Where the beam would land if the seventh disc were turned to a different angle. Not at random. Precisely. To one particular spot on the cave floor, at the foot of the great triangle, where the arrows all converged and pointed down.

He got that number too. He looked at it for a long time.

Then he tore the corner off the chart where he had written it, and folded it very small, and put it in his mouth, and chewed it, and swallowed it, the way he had seen old priests in the archives of the Patriarchate do with things that should never have been written down.

It was in his head now. It was the only place it was.

---

He stood up. He stood in the middle of the cave and looked at it, the whole of it, all at once, the way he had been trained to look at a church when he first walked in. Not the details. The shape. What the building was for.

The triangles, all pointing down. Not fertility symbols. Arrows. Every one of them pointing down, at the floor, at the rock, at the mountain, and beneath the mountain, a thousand metres down, at the sea.

The light, walking across them every day, from the spring to the autumn, touching each one, like a finger on a line of text. Like someone reading a sentence out loud, over and over, to keep it true.

And the discs, holding the light in place. So that it never missed a word.

"It isn't counting days," said Vasco slowly. "It isn't a calendar."

He turned round in the middle of the cave, looking at the arrows.

"It's holding something down."

He looked up at the channel. At the two discs left of seven.

"Every disc is a nail," he said. "And someone's pulling the nails out."

And then, before he could stop himself, the next thing.

*Alan.*

The mist on the promenade. The smell. The thing Alan had told him, on Friday morning, in the grey light, too fast, in the wrong order, the thing Vasco had been too angry to hear. The sea, coming up the steps. The old fisherman drowned in his bed.

*It's here. The thing from the cistern. It's here.*

And Vasco had said: you could have just asked me. He had said: go home.

He went out of the cave so fast he cracked his head on the low doorway and didn't feel it.

---

He ran along the cliff path to the camp, with one hand on the rope, and went straight to the radio.

He switched it on. Nothing happened.

He switched it off and on again. The dial stayed dead. No hum. No light. He knelt down and opened the metal case, and looked inside, at the neat rows of valves and wires and the little glass fuse holder where the fuse should be.

The fuse holder was empty.

He knelt there on the hot rock and looked at it for a long time.

"The fuse is gone," he said quietly.

Radios do not lose their fuses by themselves.

He thought of Yeray's face, frightened, saying: *They're not going to Las Palmas.* He thought of the steel marks round the empty sockets. He thought of Elena Suárez's last chart, the second line, the one that went nowhere near the triangles, and the word in the margin, underlined three times. He thought of Rayco's face on the first day, flat and strange, saying: *an accident in December, on the mountain road.*

He thought, very clearly: *I have not wanted to see this. I have walked past it every day for a week, because I didn't want to see it.*

A shadow fell across the radio.

"You've solved it," said a warm, tired voice behind him. "I knew you would."

Vasco turned round.

Dr. Rayco Baute was standing at the edge of the camp in his shirtsleeves and his faded field hat, against the blazing blue sky and the white sea of cloud, with the sun behind him. He was holding a thermos flask in one hand and two tin mugs in the other, and he was smiling at Vasco with enormous affection, and enormous sadness, the way a father smiles at a son who has just found out something about him.

"Coffee?" said Rayco. "Mine's better than Yeray's."

## Chapter Eight: The Vault

*Saturday, the twenty-second of June.*

They sat on a flat rock at the mouth of Cave 6, with the sea of cloud spread out below them to the end of the world, and Rayco Baute poured two cups of coffee from his thermos and handed one to Vasco, and his hand was perfectly steady.

It was better than Yeray's. It was very good coffee, black and strong, with something in it, cinnamon perhaps, and a bitterness underneath. Vasco hadn't eaten since Friday, and his hands were shaking, and he drank it without thinking, all of it, the way you drink water.

"You wrote in your thesis," said Rayco, "that a baptised bell is a voice the dead can hear. That the people who cast them believed it. That when the bell rings, it isn't calling the living to church. It's telling the dead that someone still remembers them." He looked out at the cloud. "I read it three times. I read it the week before I hired you. I thought: here is a man who understands what a building is for."

"Rayco."

"You were right. That's what I wanted to tell you. Whatever happens now. You were right about bells, and you were right about the light." He turned his cup in his cracked hands. "This is ours. The priestesses made it. Every summer for five hundred years the sun has come through that hole in the roof and said the same prayer, word by word, across the wall, and the sea has stayed down."

Vasco set his cup on the rock.

"And you're taking the prayer apart."

Rayco didn't deny it. He didn't even look surprised. He looked, if anything, relieved, the way a man looks when a doctor finally says the word he has been waiting for.

"Rayco," said Vasco quietly. "What's in the vault?"

Rayco was silent for a long moment. Down below, a single hawk was circling over the white, very small.

"In 1966," he said, "I was twenty-six years old. I had a new degree from the University of La Laguna and no power at all. None. I was a boy with a piece of paper." His voice was soft and level, the voice he used to explain things to sponsors. "My grandmother's village was a place called Tasarte, on the west coast. Above it there was a hill, and in the hill there were caves, and in the caves were our dead. My people. Canarios. Five hundred years of them. My grandmother used to take me up there when I was small and tell me their names. Not their real names. She made them up. So they'd have names."

He touched the little clay seal at his throat.

"A company bought the hill. They wanted to build a hotel. I wrote letters. I went to the town hall. I went to Madrid, on the ferry, with my own money, and sat in a corridor for three days. And in August they sent in the bulldozers, and I stood at the bottom of the hill and watched. They scraped the caves out like you'd scrape out a pot. They put the bones in a skip. My great-great-grandparents, in a skip, with the rubble. I picked some of them up off the road afterwards. I had them in a cardboard box under my bed for a year."

He was quiet for a moment.

"The site manager was a young man called Ignacio Montesdeoca," he said. "Twenty-eight. Very charming. He gave me a cigarette while I was watching, and told me not to take it personally. Now he sponsors my dig. He shakes my hand at dinners. He bought the new display cases." He smiled, a small, terrible smile. "He doesn't remember me. Why would he? I was a boy at the bottom of a hill."

"Rayco —"

"I've spent thirty years filing objections, Vasco. Thirty years. Every hotel. Every golf course. Every marina. Every road through every barranco where our people are buried. I've lost every one. Every single one." He set his cup down on the rock, very precisely. "The island has asked politely for five hundred years. It's been asking politely since 1483. Nobody has ever listened."

He looked at Vasco, and his dark eyes were full of tears, and completely steady.

"I need one number from you," he said. "Only one. Elena had it, and she's gone, and I can't do the mathematics, I never could, I'm a digger, not an astronomer. The angle for the last disc. So that on San Juan morning, at the sunrise, it sends the light the wrong way. To the door." He gestured, a small movement of one hand, out at the white sea below. "And the sea will come back for what was taken from it. The south coast. The concrete. The hotels on our graves. Not us. Not the villages. Not the people. Just the concrete."

Vasco looked at him.

He thought of Alan, in the grey light on Friday morning, saying: *the sea is coming up the steps.* He thought of a fisherman drowned in his bed in La Isleta, a long way from any concrete.

"It doesn't take orders, Rayco," he said.

Rayco smiled at him, gently, as if he were very young.

"Mine will."

---

Vasco stood up. He walked to the edge of the rock and looked down at the cloud, because he couldn't look at Rayco's face any more.

He said, without turning round, "Elena worked this out too. Didn't she."

Behind him there was a long silence.

"Where is she, Rayco?" said Vasco. "Where's Dr. Suárez?"

The silence went on so long that he turned round.

Rayco was sitting on the rock with his hands between his knees, looking at the ground. He looked suddenly very old. Older than fifty-six. He looked like the photograph in his office, the hillside, the white things on the earth.

"I took out one disc," he said. "One. In December. On the night of the twentieth, after everyone was asleep. Just to see. Just to see if it was true, what the old women in my grandmother's village used to say. That the cave was holding something." He swallowed. "She found the socket the next day. She went down the mountain that night to tell Carmen. She never got there."

"You killed her."

"I didn't touch her." His voice cracked. "I didn't touch her, Vasco. I was at the camp. I watched her lights go down the road. I went to bed." He pressed his cracked hands together. "The sea came for her. Not me. It came for her because she understood it. That afternoon she stood in that cave and read it right, for the first time, after a year of reading it wrong. And it knew." He shook his head slowly. "I didn't know it did that. That it watches. That when you loosen the lock, even a little, it comes for anyone who could shut it again. I didn't know."

He looked up.

"I know now."

Vasco stared at him.

"You know now," he said. "Then you know what it will do to the rest of them. Anyone who gets in its way. The people on the beaches. You know what's happening in Las Palmas, right now, every night. And you're still —"

"The number, Vasco."

"No."

They looked at each other.

The hawk had gone. The cloud lay under them, perfectly white, perfectly still.

Rayco nodded slowly. He picked up the thermos, and screwed the cap back on, and stood up, brushing the dust from his trousers.

"I thought you'd say that," he said. "You're a better man than me." He smiled, very tiredly. "It's very inconvenient."

---

Vasco stood up.

"I'm going down to Las Palmas," he said. "Now. I'm going to tell Carmen, and the museum, and the Guardia Civil, and anyone else who'll listen. And then I'm going to find Alan."

"I know," said Rayco gently. He didn't move from the rock.

Vasco walked across the camp to the Land Rover. The keys weren't in the ignition, where they were always left. They weren't on the hook in the finds tent either. He stood by the driver's door for a moment, looking at the empty ignition, and understood that Rayco had been down to the village this morning for more than supplies.

Then he started walking.

The dirt road went down from the camp towards Artenara, four kilometres of red dust and switchbacks and pine. In the village there would be a telephone, and a bar, and people. He had walked further than that in the Alentejo, in August, to look at a fresco.

He had gone perhaps three hundred metres when the road began to tilt.

It was very gentle at first. The pines leaned a little to the left. The red dust under his boots seemed further away than it should have been. He stopped, and put his hand on a rock, and the rock was warm and solid and very slightly moving.

The coffee, he thought. Cinnamon. And something under it, bitter, that he had been too tired to notice.

He sat down on the edge of the road, quite carefully, because his legs had decided for him. Above him the sky was enormous and blue and beginning to turn, slowly, like water going down a drain.

Footsteps on the road behind him. Two sets. One slow, one quicker.

"I'm sorry, doctor," said Yeray's voice, from somewhere very high up and very far away. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry."

"It's only to make you sleep," said Rayco's voice, gentle, close. "Elena kept it in the camp for her back. You'll wake with a headache. That's all. I would never hurt you, Vasco."

Vasco tried to say something. He wanted to say: *you hurt her.* He didn't get as far as the words.

---

He woke on stone steps, in the dark, with a headache like an iron band round his skull and the taste of cinnamon in his mouth.

Above him was a rectangle of blazing blue sky, crossed with iron bars. A grille, rusted and studded, set into a low doorway in the rock, with a shiny new brass padlock hanging from it like an earring on a corpse. And on the other side of the bars, looking down at him, two faces: Rayco's, tired and grave, and Yeray's, grey, unable to meet his eyes.

He could smell the air coming up from below him: cool, dusty, old. And under the dust, very faint, salt.

"A Canario granary," said Rayco through the bars. "Twelfth century, perhaps. They stored grain here, and cheese, and figs. It's dry. It's always been dry. That's why they chose it. You'll be safe here." He paused. "I'll come for you on Monday. After the sunrise. After it's done. You have my word."

Vasco got up onto his elbows. It took a long time.

"Your word's under a hotel, Rayco," he said.

Rayco flinched, as if he had been struck. Then he turned away, and Vasco heard his footsteps going back up the cleft, and Yeray's, slower, stopping once, and then going on. And then nothing.

---

He had a torch. It was a small pocket torch, the kind you use for reading inscriptions in dark chapels, and the battery was not new.

The granary was a long low room cut into the rock, with a rounded ceiling he could touch with his hand. It ran back into the mountain further than the torch could reach. Along the walls there were niches cut into the stone, and in the floor there were round pits with stone lids, for storing grain. The floor was made of flat stones fitted together, worn smooth by centuries of feet.

And along the left-hand wall, on the floor, in a row, there were five wooden crates.

They were the crates from the museum. He recognised them. He had signed the sheets. *Conservation transfer. Risco Caído, Cave 6.* He knelt and lifted the lid of the first one.

Inside, nested in straw, wrapped in a white cotton cloth, was a disc of obsidian. Black and perfect. He unwrapped it, and in the torchlight it gave him back his own face.

He opened them all. Five crates. Five discs.

"The missing nails," he said aloud. His voice sounded small in the low stone room. "All here."

Then he counted again.

Five here. Two left in the cave above his head. Seven, all accounted for. Tomorrow, on the eve of San Juan, Rayco would take out the sixth. And the seventh he would leave in its socket, and turn, at sunrise, to the angle Vasco had just refused to give him.

"And he thinks he can't do it without me," said Vasco.

He wondered how close Rayco could get by guessing. With six of the nails gone, perhaps close would be enough.

He sat back on his heels with the disc in his hands and listened.

Nothing. No wind. No voices. No birds. Through the rock, not a sound from the camp. He could shout until his throat bled, and the only person who would hear him was Yeray, who already knew.

Tomorrow Rayco would take out the sixth disc. On Monday at sunrise he would turn the seventh, by guesswork, and close might be enough. And down under the cloud the whole of Las Palmas would be on the beaches for San Juan, asleep on the sand among the embers of their bonfires, with the sea coming in.

And Alan. Somewhere down there under the cloud, with the sea coming in.

"Think," said Vasco aloud, to the dark. "You have until Monday. Think."

---

He didn't panic. He was a little surprised by that. He had always assumed he would. But he had spent nine years in the Church, and the Church teaches you one thing above all, which is what to do when you are frightened and alone in a dark stone room: you look at the walls.

He looked at the walls.

The torch was too weak to light the back of the granary. It made a small yellow circle, two metres across, and beyond that, nothing. He went along the walls in the yellow circle, foot by foot, with his hand on the stone.

Pick marks. The rock was covered in them, the chisel marks of whoever had cut this room nine hundred years ago. And in one place, low on the left-hand wall, half hidden behind the last crate, something else: a carving, very old, very worn, almost smoothed away. A serpent. Its body coiled round and round on itself in a tight spiral, and its head at the centre, with its mouth open.

Vasco crouched and looked at it for a long moment. He counted the coils without meaning to. Seven.

He didn't say anything. He didn't know what to say. He would think about it later, if there was a later. He stood up and went on.

Then he went back to the door.

The grille was old iron, hand-forged, the bars as thick as his thumb, riveted into a frame. The top of the frame was sunk into a lintel of hard grey basalt that a pickaxe would bounce off. The side was held by the padlock and its chain, to an iron ring set in the rock outside, where he couldn't reach. But the bottom of the frame went into the threshold, and the threshold was tuff: the soft, pale volcanic stone the Canarios had carved with stone tools. Two iron anchors, leaded into holes in it a very long time ago. The lead had gone grey and powdery.

He knelt and scratched at it with a fingernail. A little came away.

If both bottom anchors came out, the grille would still hang from the lintel and the chain. But it might swing outward at the bottom, like a cat flap. Enough, perhaps, for a thin man to get under. Might.

And the battery would not last. An hour, perhaps. Less. He turned the torch off, to save it, and sat in the dark, and thought about what he had to dig with.

---

He had almost nothing. A notebook. A pencil. A pocket torch with a dying battery.

And in his shirt pocket, where he always kept it, there was a small silver trowel.

He took it out and turned it over in the last of the torchlight. It was an archaeologist's trowel, a little one, the kind you use for delicate work, with a blade no longer than his thumb, and a handle of worn black wood. There was an inscription on the blade, very small. *L. a V. Roma.* Lorenzo to Vasco. Rome.

Lorenzo had given it to him on his twenty-seventh birthday. On the terrace of a café in Trastevere, with the dome of Saint Peter's going pink in the sunset.

"For the day you find something worth keeping," he'd said.

Vasco looked at it for a long moment.

"I thought I had," he said quietly, to nobody.

Then he turned the torch back on, just for a moment, and went to the wall beside the grille, at the height of a man's eyes, where anyone who came down those steps would see it, and began to scratch.

It took a long time. The rock was soft, but not that soft. He worked by feel as much as by sight, as the torch went yellow, and then orange. When he had finished, he held the torch up to it.

*23° 26′*

The closing angle. The number that sent the light to the heart of the great triangle and kept the door shut. Not the other one. The other one was in his head, and only in his head, and it would stay there.

Whoever came. If anyone came. Then they would know.

He looked at it. He hesitated, with the trowel in his hand.

Then, underneath it, small, he scratched one more thing. A single letter.

*A.*

He didn't know why. He didn't know if it was a signature or a message or a prayer. He only knew that it was the most he could say, after Friday, and that he needed to say it somewhere.

---

Then he knelt at the threshold, turned off the torch, and began to dig out the first anchor.

It was slow. The trowel's blade was no longer than his thumb. He worked by feel, scraping the powdery lead out a few grains at a time, then the tuff around it, then the lead again. Every so often he stopped and pushed at the bottom of the grille with his foot, and it didn't move. He went on. Hours went by, or what felt like hours; he had no way of knowing. His knees went numb, and then they went cold. And then they went wet.

He turned on the torch.

There was water on the floor of the granary. A thin black film between the flat stones, shining, spreading from the back of the room towards the steps, towards him. As he watched, it joined up and became a sheet, and touched his knees.

He dipped his finger in it and tasted it. Salt.

"Salt," he said. "In a granary. A thousand metres up."

He knelt there with the salt on his tongue, and for the first time since Friday he wasn't angry with Alan at all.

"So this is what Alan meant," he said.

Then he understood the rest of it, and the understanding was very cold.

Elena Suárez had stood in Cave 6 one afternoon in December and read it right, for the first time, after a year of reading it wrong. And the Sea had come for her that same night. Not for the other archaeologists, or the goatherds, or the people asleep in Artenara. For her. Because she knew how the door was made. Because she was the one person on the island who could have told someone how to shut it.

This morning, in Cave 6, Vasco had read it right too.

"Ah," he said quietly. "So it's me."

The water was at his ankles and rising. Slowly: a centimetre in a quarter of an hour, perhaps. The lock was still mostly holding. Five nails out of seven. Tomorrow Rayco would take out the sixth, and then it would come faster.

He turned the torch off, and bent to the anchor, and dug.

Some time in the night, kneeling in the dark in the cold rising water with a silver trowel in his hand, he did something he had not done since January, since the day he walked out of the Patriarchate with two suitcases and his grandmother's coffee pot.

He prayed.

"Pai nosso," he said, very quietly, "que estais no céu."

He stopped. The words felt rusty in his mouth, like a door that hasn't been opened in a long time.

"I'm sorry," he said, after a while. "I've been working on a Saturday. I know You're busy."

The water lapped at his thighs.

He thought of Alan. Alan on the promenade on Tuesday, white to the lips, saying *sunburn.* Alan in his cream linen suit, caught behind a desk with his hand on a map, looking like a schoolboy and a thief. Alan on Friday morning in the grey light, telling him everything, too fast, in the wrong order, too late, and Vasco not listening, because he was too frightened of what it meant that Alan had kept it from him.

Alan, whose blood it had known in the black water under Lisbon, and backed away from, and would not back away from twice.

Alan, who might be on a plane by tomorrow lunchtime, because Vasco had told him to go.

Or who might not.

"If You can't come," said Vasco to the dark, very quietly, and he was smiling, though there was no one to see it, "send the idiot."

Some time after that, the first anchor came out of the threshold with a small, wet, grinding sound, and lay in his palm like a pulled tooth.

He felt along the bottom of the frame for the second one, and began again, in the dark.

---

Fifty kilometres away and a thousand metres down, under the grey lid of the panza, in a hotel room on Las Canteras, Alan Sanches was lying on his side on the bed, in his clothes, with a rolled-up drawing on the pillow beside him, staring at a telephone that did not ring.

## Chapter Nine: The Choice

*Sunday, the twenty-third of June. The eve of San Juan.*

He packed at five in the morning without deciding to.

That was the thing he would remember afterwards, and be ashamed of, and in the end forgive himself for, a little. He didn't decide anything. He woke at five in the grey light, and got up, and his hands began to fold shirts into the suitcase from Madrid, the way they had always done, all his life, whenever things got too close. Fold the shirt. Roll the socks. Passport, wallet, ticket. The cream linen suit in its bag. He had done it in Paris and in New York and in London and in a dozen flats in Lisbon. He had done it so many times that he could do it in the dark, and his body knew how to do it without asking him.

The last thing he packed was the drawing. He rolled it carefully and put it in a cardboard tube from the hotel shop, and put the tube in the suitcase, on top.

Then he looked round the room.

Vasco's good jacket on the back of the chair. Vasco's watch on the bedside table. He picked the watch up and held it, and then he put it in his pocket. He didn't know why. To give it back, he told himself. In Lisbon. When Vasco came home.

If Vasco came home.

He went downstairs with the suitcase. Nieves was not on the desk; a young man with a moustache was, half asleep. Alan paid the bill for the room, and left a note in Vasco's pigeonhole that he wrote and rewrote four times and that said in the end only *Gone home. I'm sorry. A.*, and went out into the grey morning, and stood on the kerb on the promenade with his suitcase, and a taxi came.

"Where to?"

He had meant to say the bus station. The seven o'clock bus to Artenara. He had decided it last night, lying on the bed in the dark. He had promised it to himself.

"Gando," he said. "The airport."

The taxi pulled away along Las Canteras. On the beach the bonfires stood in their long line in the grey, waiting for tonight, with their straw figures on top in their hats. The tide was on the fifth step. Nobody was looking at it.

---

Gando airport at half past six on a Sunday in June was full of people going home. Sunburnt Germans in sandals. Sunburnt English in socks. Families with sleeping children draped over their shoulders and plastic bags full of duty-free rum. A queue of pensioners from Manchester in matching T-shirts that said WHAT HAPPENS IN GRAN CANARIA.

Alan stood in the queue for the Iberia desk with his suitcase and his ticket.

It moved slowly. He had time to think, which was the last thing he wanted.

He thought about his grandfather. Joaquim Sanches, who had been a bell founder in Lisbon, the last of them, and who had run. Alan had found out about it eight months ago, in the dark, under the city: how his grandfather had seen the Sea come once, as a young man, and had not stayed to face it. He had taken his wife and his son and got on a boat, and gone as far away from the Sea as a man can go, and spent the rest of his life pretending none of it had happened. He had been a kind man and a frightened one, and he had left his grandson nothing but a name and a box of old books and a habit.

The habit was standing in the queue at the Iberia desk with a suitcase.

Alan looked down at his hands on the handle. Founder's hands, Don Fermín had said. The men who make bells understand the things that hang.

He thought of the list in Doña Pino's missal. His own name at the bottom, in purple ink. *Now God knows too.* He thought of Pepe Santana and Agustín Ramos, drowned in their beds and their armchairs, a long way from the sea. He thought of the letter, in Inês's voice. *If it is opened in your time, and one must stand in the water, pray God it is not you.*

If he stayed, someone would have to stand in the black water at the door, and he already knew who it would be, because he could not imagine asking anyone else. The priestess had closed the door and gone down with it. If he got on the plane, he might live. He didn't know that. Perhaps the Sea could reach Lisbon; it had been there before. But perhaps it couldn't. Perhaps the Canarian lock was the Canarian sea's business, and once he was a thousand miles away, it would forget him. He might live a long time. He might go back to the shop, and Inês, and Pessoa, and sell books, and grow old, and never stand in the black water again.

And Vasco would be on the mountain. With Baute. Calling him Rayco. Not knowing.

"Next," said the woman at the Iberia desk. It was the same woman who had told him on Tuesday that his suitcase was in Madrid. She recognised him and smiled. "Señor Sanches. Your suitcase arrived! You see? Everything comes in the end."

"Yes," said Alan.

"Lisbon, via Madrid. Eleven fifteen." She took his ticket and looked at her screen. "Window or aisle?"

Alan looked at her.

Behind her, through the big glass windows of the terminal, past the runway and the scrubby brown hills, he could see the edge of the panza de burro, grey and flat, lying over the north of the island like a lid. And above it, very far away, small and brown and clear in the morning sun, the tops of the mountains.

Alan stood there for a long moment.

Then he leaned over the desk and took his ticket back out of her hand.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I've left something behind."

He picked up his suitcase, and turned, and walked back through the crowd, past the pensioners from Manchester, past the sleeping children, out through the glass doors into the heat.

Behind him, the woman at the Iberia desk looked at the empty space where he had been, and shrugged, and said to nobody in particular, "It's June."

---

By the time he got back into the city, it was half past nine, and the bus to Artenara had gone at seven.

He stood at the bus station with his suitcase and his useless ticket, and he thought, quite calmly, *Chano.*

Chano was at the kiosk, of course, selling fireworks to a queue of small boys, and he looked at Alan, and at the suitcase, and said, "You came back."

"I came back."

"From the airport."

"How do you —"

"You've got the airport face," said Chano. "Everybody comes back from the airport with the same face. Like a man who has been to the edge of a cliff and looked over." He sold a box of rockets to a child who could not have been more than eight. "What do you need?"

"Carmen Trujillo," said Alan. "From the museum. Where does she live?"

Chano didn't even pause.

"Calle Perdomo, number twelve, third floor, above the pharmacy. Her Land Rover is parked outside. It's always parked outside. It doesn't go anywhere." He looked at Alan for a moment over the rockets. "She lost her friend in December. The one who drowned in the car on the mountain. Did you know that?"

"I'm beginning to," said Alan.

---

The Land Rover was parked outside the pharmacy, half on the pavement, an ancient square thing the colour of dried mud, with a roof rack and a dented wing and a sticker on the back window that said NUCLEAR? NO GRACIAS, faded almost to nothing.

Alan left his suitcase with the pharmacist, who looked at him as if he were mad, and went up three flights of stairs, and stood outside a door with a brass knocker in the shape of a hand, and knocked.

Nothing.

He knocked again.

The door opened about four inches, on a chain. Carmen looked at him through the gap. She was in a man's dressing gown, with her black hair flat on one side and her sunglasses, for once, not on her head. Without them, her face looked bare and tired and older. There was a cigarette in her hand, unlit.

"No," she said.

"I'm an idiot," said Alan.

She stopped closing the door.

"I've been an idiot all week," said Alan. "I broke into the wrong office. I suspected the wrong man. I lied to the man I love because I thought I was protecting him, and then I lied to someone else to punish myself for it. I've been doing everything on my own because I'm frightened of needing anyone, and I've got every single thing wrong, and two people have drowned in their beds, and Vasco is up on that mountain with Rayco Baute, and I can't reach him, and I've just come back from the airport." He took a breath. "And I need your help. I can't do it on my own. I've never been able to do anything on my own. I've just been very good at pretending."

Carmen looked at him through the gap for a long time.

"Baute," she said finally.

"Not Montesdeoca. Baute. The discs never went to the city. They're in a vault under the caves. Yeray, the student, he's been carrying them down himself, every night. And only Baute has the key."

She didn't say anything. But her hand had tightened on the edge of the door so that her knuckles had gone white.

"Carmen," said Alan. "On Wednesday, in the archive. There was a photograph on your desk, face down. Two women on a mountain." He kept his voice very quiet. "One of them had a pencil through her hair."

The door closed.

He stood on the landing and thought, well, that's that, then.

Then he heard the chain come off, and the door opened again, all the way.

---

The flat was small and full of books and smelled of smoke. There were ashtrays on every surface. There were papers on the floor, and on the sofa, and on the table, and on the table by the door there was an answering machine, an old grey one, with a cassette in it, and a small red light on the top that was blinking. Slowly. Steadily. As it had blinked, Alan understood, every day for six months.

Carmen stood beside it with her arms folded tight across her dressing gown.

"Her name was Elena," she said. "Elena Suárez. She had Vasco's job before Vasco had it. She was the best reader of the cave that anyone ever had. And she was —" She stopped. "We'd been together eleven years. I was married before. To a man. A long time ago. He was very nice. It was very boring." A small, dry, terrible laugh. "She drove down the mountain on the twenty-first of December, and the Guardia found her in a dry barranco the next morning with her lungs full of the sea."

"I know," said Alan.

"She rang me first. From the bar in Artenara." Carmen looked at the blinking light. "I was out. I was at a stupid Christmas party at the museum. I got home at two in the morning and the light was blinking, and I listened to it, and I thought, oh, she's being dramatic, she'll be here in an hour. And I went to sleep." Her voice didn't break. It just stopped, for a while, and then started again. "I've never listened to it since. I can't. And I can't erase it. So it just sits there. Blinking."

Alan went and stood beside her.

"Play it," he said.

"No."

"Carmen. Please. I think she was trying to tell you what I'm trying to tell you. I think she found it first. Play it."

Carmen looked at him. Her eyes were very dry.

Then she reached out with one finger, and pressed the button.

The tape whirred. There was a click, and a hiss, and the faint background noise of a bar: a television, chairs scraping. And then a woman's voice, low and quick and warm, slightly breathless, the voice of someone who has just driven fast down a dirt road in the dark.

*Carmen. It's me. I know it's late. Listen.*

Carmen sat down on the arm of the sofa, very suddenly, as if her legs had gone.

*Somebody's taken one of the discs out of the channel. In Cave 6. The wear marks are fresh. It isn't conservation.*

Alan stood very still.

*And it isn't a calendar either. I've been reading it wrong for a year. A whole year. It's a door, Carmen. The whole cave. It's a door, and the discs keep it shut. And someone's opening it.*

A pause. A chair scraping.

*I'm driving down now. Don't smoke all of them before I get there.*

Another pause. Longer.

*Te quiero.*

Click.

The red light stopped blinking. It went steady, and then it went out.

Carmen sat on the arm of the sofa with her hands over her face. She didn't make any sound at all. Alan stood beside her, and after a while he put his hand on her shoulder, and she let him, and they stayed like that for a long time, in the small smoky room, with the sound of the city getting ready for its party coming up from the street.

---

When she took her hands away from her face, she was Carmen again. She stood up, and went into the bedroom, and came out a minute later in her rust-coloured shirt and khaki trousers and desert boots, buckling her belt, with her car keys on their carabiner, and her sunglasses back on top of her head, and an old canvas satchel in her hand.

She tipped the satchel out onto the table.

A notebook, with a battered green cover, swollen with damp. And a photograph, a large one, black and white, glossy. A police photograph. A windscreen, fogged and crusted white, and drawn on it, in salt, a triangle, pointing down.

"The Guardia gave me her things," said Carmen. "In January. They gave me the photographs too, because I asked, and because the sergeant was my cousin. I've had this notebook in this bag for six months. I've never opened it." She pushed it across the table at him. "You open it."

Alan picked up the photograph first. He looked at the triangle on the windscreen for a long time. He had seen it before. On Thursday, in the museum, behind Vasco's head, on the screen, in gold. The same shape, cut a hundred times into the wall of a cave.

He opened the notebook.

It was full of charts and diagrams and close, small handwriting, in Spanish, and most of it he couldn't follow. But at the back, on the last pages, the handwriting changed. It got bigger and faster, the way handwriting does when the thought is running ahead of the pen. And there were things there that he could follow, because he had heard them already, in a phone box, in Inês's voice.

*The discs aren't a calendar. They steer the beam. While the beam holds its path across the triangles, the lock holds.*

*Remove the discs: the beam falls wrong. The lock loosens.*

*At the first sunrise after the summer turn, the lock is weakest. If the beam falls on the door then: open.*

And underneath, circled twice:

*To close it: all seven back in the channel before that sunrise. The last one turned true. And someone has to stand in the water while it rises —*

The sentence stopped there. She had not had time to finish it.

Alan finished it for her, under his breath. "And whoever it is goes down with it."

He looked up. Carmen was watching him.

"How do you know that?" she said.

He told her. The cistern. The black water. The thing in the dark. Doña Pino's list, with his name at the bottom.

When he had finished, Carmen lit a cigarette. It took her four tries this time. She smoked half of it in silence, standing at the window, looking out over the rooftops towards the grey sea.

"So it's coming for you anyway," she said. "And someone has to stand in its water to shut it. And the last one who did didn't come back."

"Apparently."

"And you came back from the airport."

"Apparently."

She looked at him for a long moment over the top of the sunglasses, which she had lowered without seeming to notice.

"You are an idiot," she said. But it sounded different this time.

---

He called the hotel from her phone. He asked for Nieves, and Nieves was on the desk now, and he asked her whether there were any messages from Dr. Teles.

There was a pause.

"There is a message," said Nieves. "In his pigeonhole. From you, señor. It says *Gone home. I'm sorry.*" Another pause. "I have not given it to anyone."

"Burn it," said Alan.

"With pleasure," said Nieves.

"Nothing from him? Nothing at all? Since Friday?"

"Nothing, señor. I would have kept it for you." And then, in a lower voice: "Señor Sanches. Is he all right?"

Alan put the phone down without answering.

Carmen rang the museum. The security guard tried the dig radio twice while she waited. Nothing. The camp had been silent since Friday morning.

And then she rang a number Alan didn't know, and asked a quick question, and listened, and her face changed.

"Conservation lab," she said, hanging up. "I asked them how many discs from Risco Caído they've received this week. They said none. Not one. They've never had them. And there's a new line on today's transfer sheet. Disc six. Removed this morning. Signed by Rayco."

"Six," said Alan. "Out of seven."

He sat down at Carmen's table, and put his hands flat on it, on either side of Elena's notebook, and made himself think. Slowly. In order. All the way to the end. The way he had not done one single time all week.

Vasco left on Friday morning, angry, for the mountain. Vasco was the best reader of light Rayco had ever worked with. Vasco would solve the cave. He would solve it the way Elena had solved it. He would see the empty sockets and the marks of the chisel. He would understand.

And Vasco was not a man who could look at a wrong thing and say nothing. Not for long. Not when it mattered.

Vasco would have refused.

And the radio had been dead since Friday. And there was no message. Not one, in two days, from a man who left notes about the coffee.

And Yeray had said, at the bar, while Alan was busy not listening: *Down to the vault. The old granary, under the caves. And only he has the key.*

"He's in the vault," said Alan.

Carmen turned round from the window.

"Vasco refused him something," said Alan. "He must have. Baute needs something from him, something about the light, and Vasco refused. And Baute isn't a killer. He doesn't want to hurt anyone. He just wants it done. So he's put Vasco somewhere safe, somewhere he can't interfere, until it's over." He looked down at the notebook. "He's put him where he puts the things he can't let go of."

And then the rest of the letter came back to him, in Inês's voice, down a crackling line from Lisbon. *The Sea will strike first at any who understand the lock.*

Elena had read it right. And Vasco would have read it too. Of course he would. He was the best reader of light Rayco had ever had.

"Carmen," said Alan, and his own voice sounded strange to him. "It'll be coming for him."

Carmen was already picking up her keys.

---

The Land Rover took three attempts to start, which Carmen said was a good day.

They left Las Palmas at five in the afternoon, through streets already filling with people heading for the beaches, families carrying cool boxes and folding chairs and bags of fireworks, young men with crates of beer on their shoulders, a brass band in the back of a lorry playing a paso doble. Everyone going down to the sea.

The Land Rover went the other way. Up.

The road climbed out of the city through the suburbs and then through banana plantations and then through terraces of potatoes and vines, and the houses got fewer and the bends got tighter, and the grey of the panza came down to meet them. It was like driving up into the underside of a ceiling. The light went dim. The air went cold and wet. The windscreen wipers came on.

"It'll be ten minutes in the cloud," said Carmen, crunching the gears. "Then we come out on top."

The engine was making a noise. It had been making it for some time: a high, thin whine, and under it a sort of bubbling. On the dashboard, a needle that Alan didn't like the look of had been creeping steadily into the red.

"It's the radiator," said Carmen. "It does this. Pretend you can't hear it."

At the next bend, steam began to come out from under the bonnet.

They stopped in a lay-by in the grey, with the cloud pressing on the windows. Carmen got out and opened the bonnet and swore at it, comprehensively, in Canarian Spanish, for about a minute. Then she came back and opened the back door and pulled out a big plastic jerrycan of water, and a rag.

"Hold this," she said, giving Alan the rag, "and when I tell you, unscrew the cap. Slowly. If you do it fast it will boil your face off."

He did it slowly. It didn't boil his face off. It boiled his hand a little, and he swore too, in Portuguese, and Carmen poured water into the radiator, and the radiator hissed and bubbled and spat like an angry cat, and drank the whole jerrycan.

"Elena used to do this," said Carmen, screwing the cap back on. "Every time. Every single time we came up the mountain. I'd swear at it, and she'd hold the rag. She'd say, it's not the car, Carmen, it's you, it can feel you hating it." She slammed the bonnet. "She was probably right."

They went on.

And the cloud got darker.

It happened so gradually that at first Alan thought it was only the evening coming on. The grey turned to slate, and the slate to charcoal. The headlights, which Carmen had switched on, made two yellow tunnels that grew shorter and shorter. The wipers smeared something across the windscreen that was not quite rain.

And then the smell.

Salt. Cold stone. Old, deep water.

"Carmen," said Alan.

"I know," she said. Her hands were tight on the wheel. "I can smell it."

On the radio, which had been playing a football match, the commentator's voice faded, and stretched, and dissolved into static. And under the static, as Elena had heard it six months ago on this same road, the long slow hiss and draw of waves.

Alan felt the cold first in his feet.

He looked down. There was water in the footwell. Black water, round his shoes, rising. Not leaking in from anywhere. Rising, from the floor of the Land Rover, as if the floor were the bottom of the sea.

It was at his ankles. It was at his shins.

It was only on his side.

Carmen's feet, on the pedals, were dry.

"It's me," said Alan. His voice sounded strange to him, very calm and very far away. "It's come for me. I'm on the list."

The water was at his knees. It was so cold it burned, the way it had burned in the cistern. He could feel it pulling, gently, the way the sea pulls at your legs when you stand at the edge of it and a wave goes out.

He reached for the door handle. It wouldn't move.

Carmen looked at the water. She looked at Alan. She looked at the road, what she could see of it, the two yellow tunnels of the headlights in the black.

Then she put her foot to the floor.

The Land Rover roared. It was an old engine and a tired one and it had been boiling twenty minutes ago, but it roared, and leapt forward up the hill into the black, and the hairpins came at them out of nowhere, one after another, and Carmen threw the wheel round each one with both hands and her teeth bared, driving by memory, driving by fury, driving the way she should have driven down this road on the twenty-first of December if anyone had told her to.

"Not him," she was saying, through her teeth. "Not this one. Not on my road. You've had yours. Not this one."

The water was at Alan's waist. He had both hands braced against the dashboard. The salt was in his mouth.

"Carmen —"

"Hold the rag," said Carmen. "Hold the rag, Alan."

And she drove up through the black.

---

They came out of the cloud the way a swimmer comes up out of the sea.

One moment there was nothing but black and roaring and cold. The next, all at once, there was air.

Clean, cold, dry mountain air, smelling of pine. And stars.

Alan had never seen so many. The sky was full of them, from edge to edge, thick and bright and close, as if someone had spilt them. And below them, under the Land Rover's wheels, below the edge of the road, the cloud lay in the starlight like a sea of milk, flat and silver and still, all the way to the horizon.

The water in the footwell was gone. His trousers were dry. There was no salt in his mouth. There was nothing in the footwell but an old map and a crushed cigarette packet.

Carmen pulled into a lay-by and stopped, and turned off the engine, and sat with both hands on the wheel, breathing.

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

Then Alan looked down at the cloud, and saw the light.

All along the coast, far below, under the white, the cloud was glowing. Not everywhere. In patches. Small, soft, warm patches of orange, dozens of them, in a long line, following the shape of the shore, as if someone had lit lanterns under a sheet.

"The bonfires," said Carmen softly. "San Juan. They're lighting them now. Along all the beaches. The whole city's down there, by the sea."

They sat and watched the cloud glow. The whole coast of the island, burning softly orange, under a sheet of silver, under the stars.

And somewhere down there, in the dark, under the cloud, the Sea was coming in.

Carmen started the engine. It took two tries.

"Twenty minutes," she said.

---

The camp was dark when they got there. No lantern. No fire. The tents stood in the starlight like grey stones, and the museum's Land Rover was parked by the finds table with its doors hanging open.

Nobody.

Carmen turned off the engine. And in the silence, they heard it.

From somewhere below the cliff, under the caves: iron, ringing on stone. A pause. Again. And a voice, a young man's voice, shouting something they couldn't make out, high and frightened.

Alan was out of the Land Rover before it had stopped rocking.

The path went down past the dark mouths of the caves, and round the foot of the cliff, and into a cleft in the rock so narrow they had to go through it sideways, with the stone pressing on their chests, Carmen first with her torch, Alan behind her, the starlight a thin ribbon overhead.

At the end of the cleft there was a light, and the ringing of iron, and the voice.

And under it, very close, the sound of water.

## Chapter Ten: The Night of San Juan

*The night of the twenty-third to the twenty-fourth of June.*

By the evening of Sunday, the water was at Vasco's chest.

It had come faster since the morning. At dawn he had heard the chisel through the rock above his head, faint and regular, like someone knocking on a door a long way off, and known that Rayco was taking out the sixth disc. An hour later the water had begun to climb as if someone had opened a sluice.

It did not behave like water. He had stopped pretending it did. It came up out of the floor of the granary and rose around him, and wherever he went, it stood at the same height on his body. At the back of the room. On top of the stacked crates. On the top step. When he climbed, it climbed. It did not run out through the bars of the grille. It stood behind them, a flat black wall, level with his chest, and did not spill a drop.

It was not filling the room. It was filling the space around him.

He had worked all night and all day. The second anchor was nearly out. Since noon he had been working at it underwater, by feel, ducking down to scrape at the threshold and coming up to breathe, and the lead was gone, and most of the tuff around the iron was gone, and the anchor moved in its socket now, a little, like a loose tooth. When he set his shoulder to the bottom of the grille and pushed, the whole frame flexed outward, two fingers' width, three, and sprang back. The chain held the side. The lintel held the top. And the last of the anchor held the bottom, by a centimetre of stone he could no longer reach, because an hour ago the blade of Lorenzo's trowel had snapped off in the rock and was somewhere on the floor under the black water.

He stood on the top step with his face to the bars and the water at his chest and waited, because there was nothing else left for him to do.

At dusk, something happened.

Footsteps in the cleft. Slow. A light, swinging. Yeray, with a lantern in one hand and a loaf of bread and a bottle of water in the other, coming as Rayco had told him to come, every evening, so that the prisoner wouldn't starve.

He stopped three metres from the grille.

He looked at Vasco's face behind the bars. He looked at the water standing behind the bars, flat and black and level, held up by nothing. The bottle slipped out of his hand and broke on the stone.

"Mother of God," said Yeray.

"Yeray," said Vasco. "Listen to me. I haven't got long, so I'll only say it once."

The boy didn't move.

"You know what's in the cave. You carried the discs. You've seen the empty sockets. You know what Rayco's going to do at sunrise." Vasco kept his voice level. He had sat beside a hundred priests in the Patriarchate while they heard confessions, and the only thing he had ever learned from it was this: don't frighten them, don't flatter them, tell them what they already know. "And you know what this is. Water doesn't stand up on its own, Yeray. This is what killed Dr. Suárez. In her car, on the mountain road, in December. You knew her."

"She was kind to me," said Yeray. His voice was very small.

"She was. And it killed her because she understood the cave. It's killing me for the same reason. And on Monday morning, when Rayco turns that last disc, it won't stop at me. It'll go down the mountain into the city. Into La Isleta. Onto the beach at Las Canteras, where half of Las Palmas is going to be asleep on the sand." He paused. "Where does your mother live, Yeray?"

Yeray said nothing. His face was working.

"He's been like a father to you," said Vasco, gently. "I know. He was kind to me too. He still is, in his way. He made me coffee." The water touched the underside of his chin, and he tilted his head back. "I'm not asking you to betray him. I'm asking you to stop him doing the one thing he will never forgive himself for. Because he won't. You know that too."

The water was at his mouth. He took a breath while he still could.

"There's a pick by the finds table," he said. "The bottom of the grille is almost free. I've done most of it. I can't do the last of it from in here. You can."

The water closed over his mouth.

He stood on his toes on the top step, with his face pressed to the bars, breathing through his nose, and looked at Yeray, and waited. He didn't say anything else. There was nothing else to say, and the water wouldn't have let him.

Yeray dropped the bread and ran.

---

---

At the end of the cleft there was a lantern on the ground, and a grille set into the rock, and Yeray, bent double in front of it, driving the point of a pick into the stone under the bottom of the grille and heaving on the handle, and shouting, over and over, the same words: "Come on. Come on. Come on."

Behind the grille there was water.

It stood behind the bars, black and flat and level, at the height of a man's face, held up by nothing at all. And in it, pressed against the bars, a face, tipped back as far as it would go, with the water at its nose.

Alan never remembered crossing the space between them. He only remembered that he had hold of the bottom of the grille with both hands, beside the point of Yeray's pick, and was pulling, and that Carmen was behind Yeray with all her weight on the end of the pick handle, swearing, and that the iron was cold and the water coming through the bars onto his hands was colder.

Something gave. A crack, deep in the stone, like a bone breaking.

The bottom of the grille tore out of the threshold and swung outward on its chain, and Alan reached in under it, into the black water, and got Vasco by the collar, and pulled.

Vasco came out under the bars in one long slithering rush, and the two of them went over backwards together onto the stone of the cleft.

The water did not follow him.

It stood behind the grille, black and level, at the height where his face had been. It stood there for a long moment, as if it were looking at them. Then it sank. Quietly, the way it had come, down into the floor of the granary, and was gone, and in the lantern light the stones behind the bars were dry.

Vasco lay on his back on the stone, coughing up the sea.

Alan knelt over him. He had a joke ready. He always had a joke ready. It was what he did instead of feeling things. He could feel it there, waiting, on his tongue.

He didn't make it.

Vasco opened his eyes. They were very bloodshot. He looked at the lantern for a long moment without seeming to understand it. Then past it, at Alan's face.

"You came," he said. His voice was a croak.

"You sent for me," said Alan.

Something happened at the corner of Vasco's mouth that might, in better light, have been a smile.

"I asked for the idiot," he whispered. "I didn't think He'd actually send you."

Alan put his forehead down against Vasco's, and stayed there.

Behind them, Yeray had sat down on the stone with the pick across his knees, and was crying, and Carmen was standing over him with one hand on his shoulder, not saying anything at all.

---

---

They went into the granary for the discs.

It was dry. Bone dry, dusty, smelling of old stone, as if no water had ever been in it. Five wooden crates stood along the left-hand wall with their lids off, and in each, nested in straw, wrapped in a white cloth, a disc of obsidian. Carmen wrapped them in her field jacket and held them against her chest.

On the wall beside the grille, at the height of a man's eyes, where anyone who came down those steps would see it, someone had scratched into the soft stone a number.

*23° 26′*

And under it, small, a letter.

*A.*

Alan stood and looked at the letter for a long time.

---

They came up out of the cleft into the starlight and the clean cold air, and Alan wrapped Vasco in the blanket from the back of the Land Rover and sat with his arms round him on a rock at the edge of the camp, and Carmen lit the camping stove and made coffee, and Yeray stood a little way off, in the dark, not coming closer, as if he didn't think he was allowed.

Vasco drank the coffee. Slowly the shaking eased. Slowly some colour came back into his face.

"Rayco," he said. "Where is he?"

"At the cave," said Alan. "Yeray says. With a disc. Waiting for the sun."

Vasco closed his eyes.

"He took the sixth this morning," he said. "I heard him. Through the rock. I heard the chisel." He opened his eyes again and looked at Alan, and there was something in them, something fierce and clear, that Alan had not seen since the night of Vasco's presentation at the museum. "Listen to me. There are seven sockets. Six are empty now. The seventh disc is still in the channel. Rayco needs it there. He needs it turned to one exact angle at sunrise, to throw the light onto the door. He asked me for the angle. I didn't give it to him."

"The number on the wall," said Alan. "Twenty-three, twenty-six."

"That's the other one," said Vasco. "That's the closing angle. The true one. If all seven are in place, and the last one's set to that, the beam lands where the priestesses meant it to land, and the lock closes. The one Rayco wanted, I never wrote down." He touched his own forehead. "It's in here. Only in here."

"Can he guess it?"

"He'll try." Vasco's face was grim. "He'll turn it by hand, by eye, to where he thinks the door is. He won't get it exactly. He can't. But with six of the seven gone, Alan, the lock's so loose already that it might not need to be exact. Close might be enough."

"Then why can't you do it?" said Carmen. "You know the angles. You know where every one of them goes."

"Because I'll be on the floor with my eyes on the light," said Vasco. "Someone has to call every disc and every angle, to the minute, while it happens, and I'm the only one who can. And someone else has to be up that ladder. In the water."

He looked at Alan.

"It's coming for me anyway," said Alan. "I'd rather it found me doing something useful."

Nobody said anything. Vasco looked at him for a long moment, and Alan watched him understand it, and watched him decide not to say anything about it, and put it away somewhere behind his eyes.

They sat in silence. Below them, under the stars, the sea of cloud lay silver and still, glowing softly orange all along the coast where the bonfires burned.

"What time is sunrise?" said Carmen.

Vasco didn't need to look at his watch. "Seven twelve."

Alan reached into his pocket. The watch in his pocket was still going. Vasco's watch, from the bedside table in the hotel. He held it out.

Vasco looked at it for a long moment.

"You brought my watch," he said.

"I was going to give it back to you," said Alan. "In Lisbon." He swallowed. "I went to the airport this morning, Vasco. I got as far as the desk. She asked me window or aisle."

Vasco took the watch, and looked at it, and put it on, slowly, with his stiff, cold fingers, and did up the strap.

"And you said?"

"I said I'd left something behind."

Vasco didn't say anything. He looked at the watch on his wrist. Then he leaned his head against Alan's shoulder, the whole weight of it, the way he did sometimes in the mornings in Lisbon, half asleep, before the coffee.

Alan sat very still.

And there it was. Now. It was now. He could feel it rising in him, the way it had risen on Wednesday morning with Vasco's hand on his hair. The thing he had to say. *Vasco. On Friday night. After you left.*

He opened his mouth.

"Not now," said Vasco quietly, into his shoulder, without lifting his head.

Alan went cold, colder than the water.

"Whatever it is," said Vasco. "I can feel it. I can feel you carrying it. You've been carrying something since you came down those steps." He was quiet for a moment. "Not now. After. Tell me after. If there's an after. I want to hear it when I can see your face."

Alan put his arm round him, and held on.

---

They talked about Montesdeoca, because Vasco wanted Alan to understand, and it was easier than talking about anything else.

Vasco told them about Tasarte. The hill above the village, and the caves, and the bulldozers in August 1966, and the bones in a skip, and the boy at the bottom of the hill with a cardboard box under his bed for a year. And the site manager, a charming young man of twenty-eight, who had given the boy a cigarette, and told him not to take it personally, and who now bought display cases for the museum, and shook his hand at dinners, and didn't remember.

Carmen listened without moving. When he had finished, she lit a cigarette, and it took her one try.

"I knew he was angry," she said. "I studied under him. Twenty years ago. He used to stand at the front of the lecture hall and talk about the conquest, and you could see it in him, like a coal. I was angry too. I'm still angry." She looked at the glowing cloud. "But I never thought —"

"He's not wrong," said Vasco quietly. "That's the worst of it. About Tasarte. About the bones. About all of it. He's right about what was done to this island. He's only wrong about what to do."

"He's wrong about the sea," said Alan.

They looked at him.

"He thinks it'll do what he wants," said Alan. "Take the concrete and leave the people. It won't. It never does. It doesn't take orders. It does whatever it has to, to stay open. And it takes whatever's in its way." He looked down at the cloud, at the orange glow where the bonfires were, along Las Canteras, along La Isleta, along the port. "And what's nearest is them. The whole city's down there tonight. On the beaches. By the sea."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Yeray, who had been standing at the edge of the light all this time, said, in a small voice: "It's four o'clock."

Vasco got up. He swayed, and Alan caught him, and he stood, with the blanket round his shoulders.

"Then we'd better go," he said. "I'd like to talk to him first. Before the sun."

---

The path to Cave 6 ran along the cliff in the dark, with the rope for a handrail, and the sea of cloud below them lit silver by the stars.

Carmen went first, with the lantern and the discs. Then Vasco, with one hand on the rope and the other on Alan's shoulder. Then Yeray, last, carrying the pick for no reason, as if he didn't know what else to hold.

As they came round the buttress, they saw the light in the cave mouth. A small yellow glow, steady. Another lantern.

And they heard the water.

It was not the lapping of the vault. It was bigger than that, and slower. A long, deep, breathing sound, coming out of the low doorway in the cliff, in and out, in and out, like surf on a beach a long way down. Or like something very large, asleep, beginning to wake.

Alan stopped on the path.

The smell came out of the cave mouth to meet them. Salt. Stone. Deep water that had never seen the sun. Stronger than he had ever smelled it, stronger than the cistern, so strong he could taste it at the back of his throat.

Vasco's hand tightened on his shoulder.

They stooped, one by one, and went in through the low doorway.

The floor of the cave was underwater. Black water, ankle deep, lying in the round pale dome like water in a bowl, and still rising, very slowly, seeping up out of the rock between the carved stones. The lantern light shivered on it. All around them, on the walls, the hundreds of triangles pointed down at it. Every one of them. Down at the water.

And in the middle of the cave, sitting on a camp stool in the black water with a lantern at his feet, with his field hat on his knee and a black obsidian disc held loosely in both his cracked hands, was Rayco Baute.

He looked up as they came in.

He looked at Carmen, and her jacket full of discs. He looked at Yeray, who couldn't meet his eyes. He looked at Alan.

And last, he looked at Vasco, filthy and grey and wrapped in a blanket, standing in the water with his hand on Alan's shoulder.

Something moved across Rayco's face. Relief, perhaps. Or grief. Or both.

"Vasco," he said softly. "I'm so glad. I'm so glad you're not dead."

He looked down at the disc in his hands, and then up, at the channel in the roof, where the first grey of dawn was beginning to show in the slot, faint as breath on a mirror.

"You're just in time," said Rayco Baute. "Sit down. All of you. It won't be long now."

Above them, in the roof of the cave, the sky in the channel was turning from black to grey.

## Chapter Eleven: The Lock

*Dawn. Monday, the twenty-fourth of June. The feast of San Juan.*

Nobody sat down.

They stood in the black water in the round gold room, with the triangles pointing down at them from every wall, and the slot in the roof going slowly from black to grey, and they looked at Rayco Baute, and he looked back at them, quite calmly, from his camp stool.

"I turned it myself," he said, nodding up at the channel. "The last one. At four o'clock. By eye. I watched Elena do it a hundred times, and I watched you do it, Vasco, last week, when you thought I wasn't looking. I'm not a mathematician. But I'm not a fool either. I think I've got it close." He turned the black disc over in his cracked hands. "Close will do. With six of seven gone, close will do. You know that. I can see in your face that you know it."

Vasco didn't answer.

He was looking up at the channel. At the seventh disc, the only one left, black in its socket at the far end of the slot, catching the first grey light. He looked at it the way Alan had once seen him look at an altarpiece in the Madre de Deus, a long, slow, total look, taking the whole thing in.

"It's wrong," he said quietly.

"It's close."

"It's wrong, Rayco. It's turned too far. It won't go to the door. It'll go past it. Off the triangles. Off the floor. Out of the cave mouth." He turned and looked at Baute. "Do you know where that line goes, if you follow it? I worked it out yesterday, before you locked me up. It goes out of the mouth of this cave and down the barranco and north. Not south. North. It points at the city."

Rayco smiled at him, very gently, the way you smile at a child who has had a bad dream.

"The light doesn't point the sea, Vasco. It just opens the door. I'll point it. I've waited thirty years to point it. It'll come where it's called."

"It won't," said Alan.

Rayco looked at him, for the first time, properly. The bookseller. The boyfriend. The man caught behind his desk in a cream linen suit with his hand on a map.

"It doesn't come where it's called," said Alan. "It goes where it wants, and it takes whatever's in its way. A man in Lisbon tried to point it eight months ago. Vicente Lacerda. He had a reason too. A very good one. He'd lost someone he loved. And when it came, it took him, and it took half the people standing on the shore, and it had me too, and it let me go, because of whose blood I've got. I don't think it'll do that twice." He took a step forward, through the water. "It doesn't listen, Dr. Baute. It's not a weapon. It's not even angry. It's just the sea."

Rayco looked at him for a long moment. Something moved behind his dark eyes.

Then the water in the cave rose.

It rose all at once, not slowly any more: a surge, a swell, like a wave coming up a beach, from the ankles to the knees in a single breath, cold and black, and with it the sound, the long deep breathing sound from under the floor, louder, closer, the sound of something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Above them, in the slot in the roof, the grey had gone to pearl.

"Twenty minutes," said Vasco. "Twenty minutes to sunrise. Alan. The discs."

---

There were six empty sockets along the lip of the channel, and five discs in Carmen's jacket, and there was a ladder, an old wooden one, leaning against the wall below the slot, with its feet already under the water.

"Five," said Carmen, counting them out into Alan's hands. "There are only five. We need six."

Alan looked at Rayco. At the disc in Rayco's hands.

Rayco looked down at it, and his hands closed on it, tighter.

"Start with the five," said Vasco. "Quickly. I'll tell you where."

He stood at the foot of the ladder in the rising water with Elena's charts in one hand, sodden now, the ink running, and his own notebook in the other, and his eyes on the channel. He was grey with exhaustion and his legs were shaking under him, but his voice, when he spoke, was the voice from the museum. The lecturer's voice. Calm and clear and absolutely certain.

"Socket two," he said. "From the left. The one nearest the light. That one first. The flat face towards the sky, the long edge towards the back wall. Tilt it down. More. Stop."

Alan climbed the ladder with a disc of black glass in his hand.

The water came up with him. That was the strange thing, the thing he would never be able to explain afterwards to anyone who hadn't been there. As he went up the ladder, rung by rung, the water in the cave rose to follow him. Not slowly. It climbed the walls. It came up the ladder after him like a tide coming up a slipway, as if it wanted to stay with him, as if it couldn't bear to let him out of its reach. It was at his ankles on the third rung. On the fifth, it was at his knees.

And it was quiet.

The breathing sound under the floor had stopped. The cave was perfectly still. The water lay round his legs, black and cold, and did not pull, and did not surge, and did nothing at all. It only held him. The way it had held him in the vault. The way something very large holds very still when it is looking at you.

It knew him. He felt it know him. He felt it remember the cistern, and the torch, and the black water at his chest, and the moment it had tasted his blood and backed away. It was not backing away now. It was only waiting, the way it had waited in the cistern, for him to slip.

*And it drew her down with it as it went.*

Alan reached up into the channel, with the cold water at his thighs and the cold gold rock at his face, and set the first disc in its socket. It went in with a small, gritty, satisfying sound, stone on glass. He tilted it down.

"More," said Vasco from below. "More. There. Stop. Leave it."

On the wall across the cave, very faint in the grey, something shifted. A smudge of paler grey, in the place where the beam would be when the sun came. It moved a little, and settled.

"Next," said Vasco. "Socket four."

---

They did it one by one, in the order Vasco called. Socket four. Socket three. Socket six.

Once, Alan got it wrong. His hands were numb, and the disc slipped as he set it, and went in at the wrong angle, and the moment it touched the rock the water in the cave surged, all at once, roaring, up to his waist, and he heard Carmen shout below him and Yeray yell, and the whole ladder shook.

"Turn it," said Vasco, very calm. "To your right. A quarter turn. Now."

Alan turned it. The water dropped back to his knees, sulkily, like a dog called off.

He set the fifth disc in socket five.

Five. Five of the seven. The first socket, at the near end of the slot, was still empty. And at the far end, in the seventh, the last disc sat black and wrong, at Rayco's angle.

The slot above Alan's head had gone from pearl to pale gold. The sky was coming.

"Ten minutes," said Vasco.

Alan looked down from the ladder.

Rayco Baute had stood up from his camp stool. The water was at his waist. He was holding the sixth disc against his chest in both hands, the way Vasco had held the other in the channel in the vault, and he was not looking at any of them. He was looking out of the low doorway of the cave, at the sky.

At the sea of cloud.

---

It had been white all night under the stars, white and silver and still, with the bonfires glowing orange beneath it along the coast.

It was not white now.

In the north-east, where Las Palmas was, a stain was spreading across the cloud. It was black. Not grey: black, oily black, like ink dropped into milk, and it was coming up through the white from underneath, from the city, from the sea, in long dark streaks and blots that joined up as they watched, and spread. It lay over the port. It lay over La Isleta. It lay over the long curve where Las Canteras was, where the whole city had gone down to the sea last night to light its fires and jump over them for luck and wade into the water at midnight, and where tens of thousands of people were still asleep now on the sand among the embers, wrapped in blankets, with their children.

And in the south, where the resorts were, the cloud was clean.

The south of the island has no panza in June. It lay clear in the dawn, brown and gold, and the sea beyond it was blue and empty and calm, and the great white hotels of Maspalomas and Playa del Inglés stood along it in a line like teeth, untouched, with their swimming pools glittering, and nothing coming for them at all.

Rayco Baute stood in the doorway of the cave and looked at it.

He looked for a long time.

Carmen had waded over to stand beside him. She didn't touch him. She only stood there, in her rust-coloured shirt, soaked to the waist, and looked at it with him.

"That's my street," she said quietly. "Under there. Calle Perdomo. Above the pharmacy." She pointed with her chin. "That's La Isleta. That's where Doña Pino lives. She's seventy-four. That's the beach where Elena nearly drowned when she was a girl. There'll be ten thousand people on it this morning, Rayco. Our people. Canarios. Asleep."

Rayco said nothing.

"And there's your concrete," said Carmen, pointing south. "In the sun. Look at it. Not a drop."

Rayco Baute looked at the black stain spreading over the city where he was born, and the clean gold coast to the south where the hotels stood on the graves of his family, and something went out of his face.

It went out all at once, the way a candle goes out. Thirty years of it. The anger. The certainty. The coal that Carmen had seen in him, at the front of a lecture hall, twenty years ago. It just went out, and left behind it an old man standing in cold water up to his waist with a piece of black glass in his hands.

"It was never going to listen," he said. "Was it."

He turned round. He waded across the cave to the foot of the ladder, slowly, as if he were very tired. He held the disc up to Alan.

His hands were shaking.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I'm so sorry. Take it. Please. Quickly."

Alan reached down and took it.

---

"Socket one," said Vasco. "The near end. The first one the light touches. Flat face to the sky. Tilt it towards me. More. There. Stop."

Alan set it. Six.

The slot above him was blazing gold now. On the far horizon, beyond the cave mouth, beyond the edge of the white sea of cloud, a line of fire had appeared, thin and bright as a wire.

"Two minutes," said Vasco. "Now the last one. The seventh. At the far end. Alan, listen to me. You have to turn it back. Rayco's turned it too far to the right. You need to turn it to the left, and down, until it's at twenty-three degrees twenty-six minutes from the line of the channel. You can't measure it. You'll have to do it by the light. When the sun comes in, it'll hit the first disc and run down the line, and when it reaches the last, it'll go across the cave. I'll tell you where it lands. You turn the disc until it lands on the great triangle. The big one, at the back. Dead centre. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"Dead centre, Alan. Not close."

"I understand."

He climbed to the top of the ladder and reached along the slot, as far as his arm would go, and put his hand on the seventh disc. It was cold. It was colder than the others. It was colder than the water.

The water was at his chest now. On the top of the ladder, two metres off the floor of the cave, and the sea was at his chest, black and still and cold, holding him the way you hold something you are not yet sure whether to keep.

And then a cloud crossed the sun.

It was a small cloud. A wisp, high up, a scrap of white vapour drifting in from the east across the sunrise. It shouldn't have been there. The sky above the panza had been clear all night. But it was there, and it drifted across the rising sun just as the sun cleared the horizon, and the gold in the slot above Alan's head went grey.

"No," said Vasco. "No, no, no —"

The water surged. All at once. Up to Alan's shoulders. Up to his chin. The breathing under the floor came back, louder than it had ever been, a roar now, a great hollow roar like surf in a sea cave, and the whole mountain seemed to shake with it.

"Carmen!" shouted Vasco.

But Carmen was already gone.

She was out of the cave mouth, into the dawn, wading and then running along the cliff path towards the camp, and Alan heard her boots on the rock, and then, impossibly, over the roar of the water, the sound of breaking glass and tearing metal.

She came back with the wing mirror of the Land Rover in her hands. She had ripped it off its bracket. It was trailing a broken wire.

She stood in the cave mouth with her back to the cave, facing east, and held it up, high above her head, in both hands, into the light that was coming round the edge of the little cloud, and angled it, and angled it, swearing at it, the way she swore at the radiator, with the same fury and the same love.

And a beam of reflected sunlight came off the mirror and went straight up into the slot in the roof and hit the first disc.

The disc caught it. It flashed. It threw it to the second. The second to the third. The light ran down the channel from mirror to mirror like fire running down a fuse, gold, blinding, faster than Alan's eye could follow, and reached the seventh disc under his hand.

And went across the cave.

It hit the far wall, a blazing spot of gold, a metre to the right of the great triangle. Too far. On the bare rock.

"Left," said Vasco. "Turn it left. Slowly."

Alan turned the disc. Under his numb fingers, against the cold, it moved, stiffly, with a faint gritty sound. On the far wall, the gold spot slid left. Across the rock. Onto the edge of the great triangle.

"More. Slowly. Slowly. And down."

The water was at his mouth. He tipped his head back to breathe. The salt was on his lips. The cold had gone into his hands and he couldn't feel them, he could only see them, in the gold light, on the black disc, turning.

"Down. A little more. A little more."

The spot of gold crossed the great triangle, slowly, from the edge to the middle.

The little cloud slid off the face of the sun.

The real light came. The full light, the first light of the twenty-fourth of June, pouring in through the slot in the roof like water through a broken dam, and it hit the first disc, and ran down the channel, through all seven, gold on black on gold, and came out of the seventh and went across the cave, and the spot from Carmen's mirror vanished in it like a candle in the sun.

"There," said Vasco. "There. Stop. Alan. Stop. Don't move. That's it. That's the one."

Alan took his hand off the disc.

The beam lay across the cave. A single straight blade of gold, from the roof to the back wall, so bright that the air itself seemed to burn. And at the end of it, on the great triangle, a metre high, pointing down, at the very centre, where every line of every carving in the whole cave converged, a spot of light the size of a hand.

Dead centre.

Twenty-three degrees. Twenty-six minutes. The tilt of the world.

---

For a moment nothing happened.

The cave was gold. Every triangle on every wall was lit, all at once, every cut line of every one of them blazing gold and black, as if the whole room had been written on in fire. The water lay round Alan, still, up to his chin, black, and the gold light lay on it, and it did not move.

Then it breathed out.

That was the only way he could ever describe it. A long, slow, vast exhalation, through the rock, through the mountain, through the whole island. A sound like a breath let go after it has been held for a very long time. And the water began to go down.

It went down the way it had come, back into the rock. And it took Alan with it.

He felt it first as a tug at his legs, the way a wave tugs when it goes out. Then it was not a tug. The whole black weight of it was going down, through the floor of the cave, through the door at the foot of the great triangle, back to wherever it lived, and it was taking what it held. It pulled him off the top of the ladder as easily as a hand pulls a weed. He went into the water, and under, and down.

Black. Cold. The roar of it in his ears, and the pull, down and down, and below him, where the stone floor of the cave should have been, there was no stone. Only more dark, opening.

*So this is how she went,* he thought, quite calmly. *Alone in the water, when the door shut.*

He stopped fighting. The light was on the triangle. He had done it. Nothing else mattered.

And a hand closed on his wrist.

It closed hard enough to hurt. It closed the way a man holds a rope over a cliff. The water pulled, and the hand held. The water pulled harder, and the hand held, and somewhere above him in the dark someone was shouting, and someone else had hold of the man who was shouting, and someone else had hold of them: a chain of hands, in the black, against the sea.

Then, all at once, the pull was gone.

The water drained away round him, sinking, softly, into the floor, into the mountain, into the dark, the way a wave draws back down a beach, and he was lying on dry stone at the foot of the great triangle, in the gold light, coughing up the sea.

Vasco was kneeling over him with Alan's wrist in both hands. Behind him Carmen had her fists knotted in the back of Vasco's shirt. Behind her, soaked to the shoulders, was Yeray. And behind Yeray, with both arms locked round the boy's waist, was Rayco Baute.

Nobody let go for a long time.

"You stopped," said Vasco. His voice shook. "I saw your face. You were letting it take you."

Alan couldn't answer.

"I've let go of people before," said Vasco. "I got on a train once, in the middle of the night, and let go of the best man I ever knew." His hands were white on Alan's wrist. "I wasn't going to do it twice."

Later, when they could stand, Carmen went to the mouth of the cave with the broken wing mirror still hanging from one hand, and made a sound, and they all went to look.

Beyond her, the sun was up. It had cleared the horizon and stood above it, enormous, gold, blinding, over the sea of cloud.

And the cloud had torn.

It does that, sometimes, at sunrise in June. The warmth of the sun on the top of the panza thins it, and for a few minutes it opens in rifts and holes, and through them you can see down, a thousand metres, to the sea.

Through one of those holes, far below, at the bottom of the white, the sea lay flat and blue and glittering in the first sun.

And for a moment, only a moment, beneath the glitter, something moved.

Alan saw it. He thought, afterwards, that they all saw it. Something very large. Much larger than a ship, or a whale, or anything that has a name. A long dark shape under the bright water, turning, slowly, the way a sleeper turns over, and settling, and going down, and down, into the blue, until it was gone.

The surface of the sea closed over it, flat and shining, and the cloud closed over the sea.

Nobody said anything. Nobody ever said anything about it, afterwards, to anyone. Not one of them.

---

The sun climbed. The sea of cloud lay below it, white again, from edge to edge. In the north-east, over the city, the black stain was sinking back down through the white, thinning, fading, like ink dissolving in water, until there was nothing left over Las Palmas but the ordinary, flat, harmless grey of the panza de burro, the donkey's belly, the cloud that comes every June.

Down there, under it, ten thousand people were waking up on the beaches, among the ashes of their bonfires, with sand in their hair and hangovers and children to feed, on the morning of the city's five hundred and eighteenth birthday, without the faintest idea that anything had happened at all.

In Cave 6, on the morning of San Juan, the light walked slowly across the wall, from triangle to triangle, touching each one, like a finger moving along a line of text. Like someone reading a very old sentence out loud, word by word, to keep it true.

Vasco sat beside Alan on the dry floor with his back against the wall. He was still holding Alan's wrist. He didn't seem to know he was doing it.

Alan didn't tell him.

## Chapter Twelve: The Confession

*Monday, the twenty-fourth of June. Las Palmas is five hundred and eighteen years old.*

They came out of the cave into the sun.

It was the first time Alan had stood in it, properly, since he landed. Not the grey heat under the panza, but the sun itself, enormous and close and gold, pouring down on the red rock of the cliff and the green pines and the white sea of cloud, so bright that he had to close his eyes, and stood there with them closed, soaked through, shaking, feeling it on his face.

Vasco had been right. It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful place he had ever stood.

Carmen had sat down on the flat rock at the cave mouth with the Land Rover's wing mirror in her lap, trailing its wire, and was lighting a cigarette. It took her one try. She looked at the lighter in surprise.

Rayco Baute sat a little way off on the rock, with his hat in his hands, looking at the cloud. He didn't move. He looked like a man who had been carrying something heavy up a mountain for thirty years and had just put it down and didn't know what to do with his arms.

Yeray stood at the edge of the path, holding the pick.

And Vasco was beside Alan, in the blanket, with his wet hair standing up and his face grey and lit, looking at the sea of cloud as if he had never seen it before.

Alan opened his eyes.

He had said he would wait. Vasco had asked him to. *After. If there's an after. When I can see your face.*

There was an after. It was this. It was the sun, and the rock, and the cloud, and Vasco's face in the light.

And he could feel himself beginning to do it again. The old thing. The thing he always did. Choosing the moment. Waiting for a better one. Later, when Vasco's rested. Later, when we're off the mountain. Later, in Lisbon, at home, with a drink. Later, when I've worked out how to say it so it doesn't sound so bad.

*Tell him. Today. Not when it's convenient.*

"Vasco," said Alan.

Vasco turned and looked at him.

And Alan told him. Before he could ask. Before he could say *what is it,* or *not now,* or anything at all. He told him plainly, in the sun, with Carmen and Rayco and Yeray all near enough to hear and none of them looking, the way people don't look at something that isn't theirs.

"On Friday night, after you left, I went out," said Alan. "I went to a bar in Vegueta. Rafa's bar. I drank too much rum. And I met someone, the barman, Rafa, and I slept with him." His voice didn't shake. He was surprised by that. "He asked me if there was someone. Straight out. And I said no. I said it twice."

Vasco didn't move.

"I broke both of them," said Alan. "Both your rules. I didn't tell you first. And I did it because we'd had a fight. Because you'd left. Because I was angry and frightened and I wanted, for one night, to be somebody who had nothing to lose. I did it on purpose." He swallowed. "And then I lied to him about you. And I would have lied to you about him. I was going to. On Saturday morning, I was already working out how."

He stopped. There was nothing else. That was all of it.

He didn't say *I was drunk.* He didn't say *you'd left me.* He didn't say *it meant nothing,* because it hadn't meant nothing, and he wasn't going to lie about that either.

He just stood there in the sun and let Vasco look at him.

Vasco looked at him for a very long time.

Above them a hawk was turning, very high, in the blue. Below them the cloud lay white and still. The cigarette in Carmen's fingers burned down, unsmoked.

"Yeray told me," said Vasco at last, very quietly. "On Saturday morning. At the camp. He said there was a Portuguese guy at Rafa's bar on Friday night. Funny. Sad underneath. Like a man at his own party who doesn't know why he came."

Alan closed his eyes.

"I told myself it couldn't be you," said Vasco. "I told myself there are a great many Portuguese men in Las Palmas. I lay in that channel in the dark all night, and I told myself that over and over. And I knew. Of course I knew." He was quiet for a moment. "And then you came down the steps into the water to get me. And I thought, if he tells me, he'll tell me before I ask. And if he doesn't, he'll wait until I do."

He looked at Alan.

"You didn't wait," he said.

"No."

Vasco looked down at his hands. At his watch, on his wrist, that Alan had brought up the mountain in his pocket.

He didn't say *it's all right.* He didn't say *I forgive you.* Alan hadn't expected him to, and he would have been frightened if he had. Vasco wasn't a man who said things he didn't mean yet.

What he said was this.

"Thank you for telling me first."

And he didn't move away.

They stood there side by side on the rock in the sun, not touching, a hand's breadth apart, the way they had lain in the hotel bed on Thursday night. But it was different. Alan didn't know how. Only that it was. As if something that had been held under the surface for a very long time had been let go, and had drawn back, and was gone.

After a while, Vasco reached into his shirt pocket, and took something out, and held it out to Alan on his palm.

It was a small silver trowel, with a worn black handle and half a blade. There was something engraved on the blade, very small.

"Lorenzo gave me this," said Vasco. "In Rome. On my twenty-seventh birthday. He said: 'For the day you find something worth keeping.'" He looked at the trowel. "I kept it for twelve years. I kept it in my pocket through the whole of the Church. I used it in the vault to write on the wall, and to dig my way out, and I broke it." He held it out a little further. "I don't need it any more."

Alan looked at it.

"For the day you find something worth keeping," said Vasco. And then, after a pause, very quietly: "Keep it."

Alan took it. It was warm from Vasco's pocket. He closed his hand round it, and put it in his own, and his hand stayed on it there, through the wet cloth, for a long time.

---

Yeray had the fuse.

He produced it from the pocket of his shorts, wrapped in a twist of paper, and held it out to Carmen without a word, and she looked at it, and then at him, and then took it and walked to the camp and put it back in the radio and switched it on.

The radio hummed. The dial lit up.

She called the museum, and the museum called the Guardia Civil, and Carmen spoke to someone in the Guardia Civil who turned out, after some confusion, to be her cousin, the sergeant, the one who had given her the photographs in January. She talked to him for a long time, in a low voice, with her back to the others.

When she switched off, she went over to Rayco, who was still sitting on the rock with his hat in his hands.

"They'll be here in an hour," she said. "From Artenara. I told them you'd be waiting."

Rayco nodded. He didn't look up.

"I'll wait," he said.

Carmen stood looking down at him. Her old teacher. The man at the front of the lecture hall with the coal burning in him.

"I'll come down with you," she said. "In the car. I'm not letting you go down that road on your own."

Rayco looked up at her then. His dark eyes were wet.

"Carmen," he said. "Elena —"

"I know," said Carmen. "I know, Rayco. Not now." She sat down on the rock beside him. Not close. But beside him. "There'll be time for that. I'll visit. I'll bring you cigarettes. I'll shout at you through the glass. Every week. For as long as it takes." She lit a cigarette and gave it to him, and lit another for herself. "You were right about Tasarte. You were right about all of it. Somebody has to keep saying it. Properly. In a way that doesn't drown anyone." She blew out smoke at the cloud. "Looks like it's going to have to be me."

They sat side by side on the rock and smoked, and looked at the sea of cloud, and didn't say anything else.

After a while, Carmen took the Land Rover keys off their carabiner and threw them, without looking, over her shoulder.

Alan caught them.

"Bring it back to the flat," she said. "Calle Perdomo. Above the pharmacy. Fill the radiator at the bottom of the hill. It doesn't have a left wing mirror any more. Don't let it see you hating it."

---

The Guardia came at nine, in a white Land Rover that was even older than Carmen's, two young officers who looked at Rayco Baute with a mixture of confusion and awe, as if they had been sent to arrest a mountain. He went with them quietly. He shook Vasco's hand at the door of the car, and held it, and didn't let go for a long moment.

"You'll make a fine director one day," he said. "Of something. Don't let them put you in a museum."

Then he got in, and Carmen got in beside him, and Yeray, who had made the first real decision of his life the evening before, with a pick, in front of a grille, got in the front, and the white Land Rover went away down the dirt road towards Artenara in a cloud of red dust.

And Alan and Vasco were alone on the mountain.

---

They drove down in the afternoon.

Alan drove. Vasco was too tired, and had a lump on his forehead the size of a quail's egg from the doorway of Cave 6 that he still couldn't remember getting. The Land Rover started on the third try, and boiled once, at the top of the first hill, and they stopped and filled the radiator from the jerrycan, and Alan held the rag, and Vasco swore at it, comprehensively, in Portuguese, until it stopped.

Then they went down into the cloud.

It was only cloud. Grey and cool and damp, ten minutes of it, the windscreen wipers going, the headlights making two short yellow tunnels. It smelled of pine and wet earth. There was no salt in it at all. Nothing in the footwell but an old map and a crushed cigarette packet.

Alan drove through it slowly, with both hands on the wheel, round the hairpins, down and down.

At the bottom of the cloud, where it thinned and broke, and the grey city began to show through it far below, and the sea beyond it, flat and ordinary and pewter-coloured, he changed gear for the next bend, and Vasco reached over and put his hand on Alan's, on the gearstick.

And left it there.

Alan drove with Vasco's hand on his. Down through the last of the cloud, out into the grey afternoon, through the potato terraces and the vines and the banana plantations, towards the city. He didn't say anything for a long time. He didn't want to say anything that might make the hand move.

Then, at last, because he had promised himself he would never again not say a thing because he was afraid of the answer, he said:

"Vasco. Are we — I mean, after this. Are we going to be —"

He didn't know how to finish it. He didn't know what word went there.

Vasco looked out of the window at the grey city coming up to meet them. At the long curve of Las Canteras, and the sea. And beyond the sea, somewhere, very far away, beyond the end of the world, on the other side of the ocean, along the old sail route that every ship had taken from Lisbon to the Americas for five hundred years, the next island.

"Ask me again in Santo Domingo," said Vasco.

And his hand stayed on Alan's on the gearstick, all the way down.

---

The city was having its birthday.

The streets were full of people, and music, and smoke from food stalls, and flags, and brass bands, and children with sparklers, and old men in their best suits, and young men in nothing much at all. Five hundred and eighteen years since Juan Rejón had come up the Guiniguada ravine and planted a flag in the mud and called it a city, on the morning of San Juan, on the morning the sun turns back.

Nobody knew how nearly it hadn't had a five hundred and nineteenth.

Nieves was behind the desk in the lobby when they came in. She looked at them both. At Vasco's forehead, and the blanket he was still wearing, and Alan's suit, which had been through rum and turpentine and a flooded granary and a flooded cave and would never, he suspected, be the same again.

She looked at them for a long time.

Then her eyebrow went up. All the way up. As far as it would go.

And she smiled. Alan had never seen her smile. It transformed her entirely.

"Welcome back, Dr. Teles," she said. "Señor Sanches. There are no messages. I burned the one there was." She held out their key. "There is fresh coffee in the machine. I made it myself. It is still terrible."

---

Chano was at his kiosk on the corner, selling the last of the fireworks, and he waved a box of rockets at them as they came out onto the promenade at dusk.

"You found him," he said to Alan.

"I found him."

"Up the mountain."

"Up the mountain."

Chano nodded, satisfied, as if this were exactly what he had predicted all along. Then he leaned forward over the counter and lowered his voice.

"My cousin's husband's brother," he said, "was out on his boat at sunrise this morning. Off Agaete. Fishing. And he swears to God, on his mother's grave, that he saw San Borondón." He crossed himself. "The island. The one that's only there sometimes. He says it came up out of the sea at sunrise, right there off the coast, green, with trees on it, and mountains, and then the sun came up and it was gone." He shrugged. "Of course, he's a liar. Everybody knows he's a liar. But still." He looked out at the sea. "Strange day."

"Strange day," agreed Alan.

---

They watched the fireworks from the balcony of the hotel room.

They went up from the beach at midnight, from all along Las Canteras, from the port, from La Isleta, from the old town, hundreds of them, gold and red and green and white, bursting under the grey lid of the panza so that the whole cloud lit up from underneath like a lampshade, flickering, glowing, every colour at once. The noise came across the water a second late, a long rolling thunder. On the beach below, people were cheering, and somewhere a brass band was playing, and somewhere else someone was singing a song about a woman who had gone to Venezuela and never come back.

Alan stood at the rail and watched them.

Vasco came and stood beside him.

They didn't say anything. They didn't need to. They stood together on the balcony and watched the city's birthday light up the cloud, and below them the sea came in and went out on the sand, quietly, harmlessly, the ordinary sea, the way it had every night for five hundred years.

After a while Vasco's hand found Alan's on the rail.

And later, much later, when the fireworks had stopped and the beach had gone quiet and the only sound was the sea, they went inside.

It was not like Tuesday night. It wasn't easy, and it wasn't funny, and it wasn't familiar. They were both too tired and too careful and too aware of everything that had happened, and of everything that hadn't been mended yet, and might not be for a long time. It was quiet. It was slow. It was two men who knew exactly what they had nearly lost, choosing, very deliberately, in the dark, not to lose it.

It wasn't forgiveness. Neither of them pretended it was.

It was a choice.

And afterwards, Vasco didn't fall asleep at once, the way he usually did. He lay awake, with his head on Alan's chest, and Alan lay awake with his hand in Vasco's hair, and they listened to the sea together until the grey light came.

---

In the morning, when Alan woke up, Vasco had already gone down to the lobby.

The balcony doors were open. The grey light of the panza lay over the room, warm and close, the way it would every morning until July. The ceiling fan went round. Down the corridor, the lift sneezed.

And on the bedside table, on Alan's side of the bed, there was a cup of coffee.

It was from the machine in the lobby. It would be terrible.

Alan lay there and looked at it for a long time.

Then he sat up, and reached for it, and drank it.
