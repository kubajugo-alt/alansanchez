# Alan Sanches II: The Shortest Night — Story Bible and Scenes

·

The second Alan Sanches game, set on Gran Canaria in June 1996, eight months after The Drowned Bell (October–November 1995). It's the first stop on the old sail route to the Americas: Lisbon, Gran Canaria, Santo Domingo, St. Augustine. The later games follow that route and end with Alan's mother in St. Augustine.

**What changed from the first brief.** Alan is a bookseller out of his depth again, not an investigator. Vasco is perceptive, not naive, and has left the Church's employ. Inês is back, on the phone from Lisbon. Magec is the sun that binds the Sea, not the Sea itself. The Sea is the same one Lisbon's bell called, and it can't be aimed. Baute's cause is treated as right and only his means as wrong. Vasco half-rescues himself. Ansite and Risco Caído history is corrected.

## Premise

**Logline.** Eight months into the first relationship he's ever stayed in, a Lisbon bookseller follows his archaeologist boyfriend to Gran Canaria, wrecks his career in a day, sleeps with the wrong man, and has one week, and the shortest night of the year, to put back the lock that has held the Atlantic since 1483.

**The story in one paragraph.** Vasco has left the Patriarchate for his first job outside the Church: the sacred-light specialist on Dr. Rayco Baute's 1996 expedition to Risco Caído, a cave sanctuary in the mountains where light falls through the rock. Alan comes along, bored, jealous of the work and frightened by how much he wants to stay. He notices fog that doesn't belong in a Canarian June and recognises it from Lisbon. Nobody listens, so he breaks into Baute's office, gets caught, and humiliates Vasco in front of the people he came to impress. Vasco goes up the mountain alone. Alan spends the night with Rafa, a young painter in Vegueta, and wakes up knowing exactly what he's done. Meanwhile Vasco solves the cave's light channel, and Baute uses the solution to open what the Canario priestesses closed. Alan has to choose whether to run, then get to the mountain with a chain-smoking archivist in a dying Land Rover, and face the Sea a second time. The finale is two people solving a lock together. The ending is a confession.

## Setting and time

**When.** Tuesday 18 to Monday 24 June 1996: the week of the Fiestas de San Juan and Las Palmas's 518th birthday (founded 24 June 1478). The climax is the Noche de San Juan, 23–24 June, the night of bonfires, two nights after the solstice.

**Where.** Las Palmas (Vegueta, Triana, La Isleta, the port), and the mountain interior: Artenara and Risco Caído, reached by hairpin roads above the clouds.

**The contrast.** Midsummer heat, festival banners, fireworks being tested, beach crowds, and against it, things that shouldn't be: tides arriving at the wrong hours, salt on the stones of mountain caves, whispering in the surf, and a black mist that rolls in at dusk under the ordinary grey of the panza de burro.

**The panza de burro.** Every June the trade winds pile a low, flat ceiling of grey cloud over Las Palmas and the north of the island: the "donkey's belly". Below it the city is warm, humid and grey for days; above it, the mountains stand in blazing sun. It gives the game its two worlds. Down in the city, under the cloud, is the Sea's world: damp, close, grey, where the wrong mist hides in plain sight. Up the mountain, through the cloud and out into the light, is Magec's world. Every climb to Risco Caído is a passage from one to the other.

**Why nobody notices.** Islanders dismiss the black mist as panza de burro. "It's June, cariño. It's the donkey's belly." Only Alan, who has smelled it before, knows the difference: the panza is high, grey and still; the mist is low, black, smells of salt, and moves against the trade wind.

**The clock.** Each night the mist comes further inland, and each day the tide reaches higher. The San Juan bonfires on the beaches are the game's countdown: the city is building fires to welcome the shortest night, unaware what it's welcoming.

## Lore: the Sea in the Canaries

The Lore of the Sea from the first game still holds: the Sea is ancient, indifferent and never shown; it answers when called, keeps bargains to the letter, and comes as water. Gran Canaria adds the oldest chapter of that story.

**1483: the calling.** At the spring equinox of 1483, as Castile closed in, the Harimaguadas, the sacred women of Gran Canaria, gathered on the cliffs above Gáldar, where the Castilian ships lay at anchor. Desperate, they did what the Lisbon widows would do 270 years later: they called the Sea to take the invaders' ships. It came, black, against the wind. It took the ships, then the coast and the royal camp, then their own villages, and it kept coming every night, into low places and beds, for three months. It can't be aimed. The priestesses went up to Cave 6 at Risco Caído, the almogarén (sanctuary), and at sunrise on 24 June 1483 they shut it out again. The Castilians, who saw only that the sea went back on Saint John's morning, called it a miracle of the Baptist.

**The binding: Magec.** To stop it, the priestesses turned to the one power the Sea has never answered to: Magec, the sun. The cave's ceiling channel already let a beam of summer sunlight cross the cave and fall across the engraved triangles on its walls. They set polished obsidian discs in the channel to fix the beam's path **(fiction)**. Each summer the light renews the lock, like a prayer said by the sun itself. For five hundred years, the Sea off Gran Canaria has slept.

**Who can close it.** Anyone can put the discs back, but it has to be done from inside the Sea's water, at the door, at the turning sunrise, while it rises. Whoever does it is likely to drown: when the door shuts, the water drains back through it and takes whatever is standing in it. The last priestess closed the lock in 1483 and went down with it. In 1996 Vasco has to read the light and call every disc from the floor, so someone else has to climb into the water. Alan chooses to. The Sea is coming for him anyway (in the Lisbon cistern it knew him for the bell-makers' blood and backed away; with a door opening, that blood, the blood that bound it in Lisbon in 1755, is what it fears most), and he is afraid from the first night.

**What Baute is doing.** Baute believes the Sea is the islanders' ancestral weapon and that it can be aimed. Released on the night of San Juan, he thinks, it will take back the south coast resorts built over Canario burial grounds, and nothing else. He's wrong in the same way Vicente Lacerda was wrong. The Sea can't be pointed. It keeps its own accounts: it collects what it is owed, and beyond that it takes whatever is nearest: the port, La Isleta, Vegueta, his own people first.

**How the lock opens.** Over the week Baute's team removes the obsidian discs one by one, calling it conservation. Each disc removed loosens the lock: the mist comes, the tides go wrong. Six come out. The seventh has to stay: the lock opens completely only if, at the San Juan sunrise, the last disc throws the beam onto one precise spot, the door. Baute can't calculate that angle; Elena could, and Elena is dead. Vasco's reading of the channel gives him both numbers: the angle that opens the lock, which he refuses Baute, and the angle that closes it, 23° 26′.

**Why San Juan.** Risco Caído's light display is usually tied to the equinoxes, and runs through the summer. The lock isn't about the equinox; it's about the turn. The beam climbs the walls until the solstice, when the sun "stands still": for about three days its rising point barely moves. The priestesses held that the lock is weakest at the first sunrise when the light visibly starts back, the fourth morning, which falls on 24 June. (A "turning" in the series' sense is those few days either side of a solstice or equinox, when the sun seems to stand still: the window in which the Sea can be called, and a loosened lock can be broken.) Rome counted the same way: the Julian calendar put the summer solstice on 24 June, and the Church set the feast of Saint John there. Vasco explains it on the mountain: "The Church put Saint John on the day the sun turns back. The priestesses got there first."

**What the living see.** Black mist rolling in at dusk beneath the panza de burro, against the wind. Tides at the wrong hours. Whispering in the surf at Las Canteras. Salt crystallising on the walls of a dry mountain cave. La Isleta's fishermen hauling up things the sea has kept since 1478: a corroded helmet, a cannonball, a crossbow bolt. The Sea is giving back what it took, and nobody knows why it's emptying its pockets.

**The Lisbon thread.** In 1751 the Lisbon founders came to Las Palmas to study the Canarian lock, because they were about to cast a bell for the widows and were afraid of what it might call. They learned its rules and cast their first bell here, for Santa Ana, safe behind the priestesses' lock. They didn't understand in time that the lock was the only thing making it safe. They went home and cast the widows' bell the same way; in 1755 it called the Sea, and what they had learned here is how they knew to bind it afterwards. In Santa Ana cathedral Alan finds the 1751 bell with the Sanches mark: his family were here before him. This is required: together with the founders' letter (decoded on Saturday), it's how Alan learns the lock can be closed, and what it costs.

**Handle with care.** The conquest, the destruction of Canario burial sites and the cost of mass tourism are real and still felt on the island. The game takes them seriously: Baute's grievance is legitimate. Only his means are monstrous.

## Characters

### Alan Sanches (38)

Still a bookseller. He has left the shop with Inês for a week and moved Vasco into the flat above it. Committing once, on a Thursday morning in a casting pit, turned out to be easy. Staying is hard. Eight months of shared mornings have him panicking: about grey hairs, about being needed, about the fact that someone now notices when he's late.

His old flaw, avoidance, comes back dressed as independence. He acts alone because asking for help means admitting he depends on someone. He doesn't break into Baute's office because he's a rogue; he does it because telling Vasco "I'm scared something's wrong" feels more dangerous than a burglary. Under all of it, from Tuesday night, he is afraid for his life: the Sea knew his blood in the Lisbon cistern, and he knows it has come for him.

**The rule.** Since the first game they've been open, on two conditions Vasco set and Alan agreed to: tell each other first, not after; and never to settle a fight. The Rafa night breaks both. The sex was never the problem; the anger and the silence are.

**Arc:** from "I committed" to "I stay": through the fight, the night with Rafa, the choice to go up the mountain, and the confession.

### Vasco Teles (39)

In January he left the Patriarchate. This is his first job outside the Church: the sacred-light specialist on Baute's expedition. His whole career has been churches built to face the sunrise, so a cave that catches the sun is the work of his life. His reputation in his new world rests on this week.

He isn't naive. He trusts Baute because Baute loves the dead the way he does, and because he has wanted a mentor since Rome. He sees Baute's flaws late because he doesn't want to see them. His own flaw is that he swallows anger until it detonates.

**What he wants from Alan:** to show up, reliably, without being asked. **What he finally tells him** in the fight: about Lorenzo, the man he left in Rome out of fear. "I've been the one who leaves. I won't be left by a man who's scared."

### Inês Carvalho (34), on the phone

In Lisbon, minding the shop and Pessoa, and three weeks from submitting her thesis, which she is avoiding by researching Alan's case. She is Alan's source on the Sea and the founders. By phone and fax she digs through his grandfather's boxes and finds the foundry ledger (the 1751 voyage "to study the lock"; the 1755 page, "We did what we learned in the Canaries. The Sea is silent."), tells him the Sea knows his blood, and decodes the founders' letter. She's the one Alan calls the morning after Rafa. She doesn't absolve him: "Tell him. Today. Not when it's convenient."

### Dr. Carmen Trujillo (42)

Senior archivist at El Museo Canario. Canaria born and bred, divorced, chain-smoking, unshockable, with a 1980s Land Rover held together by faith. She studied under Baute and shares his anger about what the island has lost; she just won't drown anyone over it. That makes her the game's most important voice: she says Baute is right about the injustice and wrong about everything else.

She isn't another Inês. Inês loves Alan and scolds him. Carmen barely likes him, tells him he's an idiot to his face, and helps anyway, because what Alan describes is what Elena died describing. She is Alan's source on the Canarios, and he comes back to her archive almost every day: the harimaguadas, the conquest, the legend of 1483, how the cave works, and finally Elena. She also sends him to Casa de Colón for the Castilian side. **Arc:** from a cynic who's stopped fighting to the woman who drives up the mountain in the dark.

### Dr. Rayco Baute (56)

Director of the Risco Caído expedition. His family claims descent from Canario nobility. In 1966, aged twenty-six and newly graduated, he watched bulldozers flatten the burial ground above his grandmother's village for a hotel. He became an archaeologist to protect what was left and has lost every fight since, to planning permits, developers and indifference.

He believes the Sea is the island's justice. He isn't cruel: he respects Vasco, needs his reading, and locks him in the lower vault only because Vasco understands too much and refuses to give the final angle. He's the mirror of Vasco (both love the dead) and of Vicente Lacerda (both think grief entitles them to the Sea).

**Fate:** unlike Vicente, he lives. When he sees the Sea heading for Las Palmas, not the resorts, he understands. He gives Alan the sixth disc and, when the lock shuts, holds on to Yeray in the chain of hands that keeps Alan out of the door. He ends the game turning himself in; Carmen rides down with him in the Guardia car, and later visits.

### Rafa (Rafael, 23)

Painter and bartender in Vegueta, ink on his hands and charcoal in his pockets. Free, funny and unafraid, he's Alan at twenty-three, the life Alan thinks he misses. He isn't a homewrecker. On the night it happens he asks "Is there someone?" and Alan lies. **The lie is Alan's sin**, more than the sex. In the morning Rafa has drawn Alan asleep. When Alan tells him the truth over coffee, he gives Alan the drawing anyway: "Take it. It's the last time you'll look that young." This is his only appearance in the series.

## Character designs: Carmen, Rafa and Rayco

All three in the first game's painted style, readable at sprite size. Each has a clear silhouette, a palette that doesn't clash with Alan and Vasco, and one signature gesture for idle animation.

### Dr. Carmen Trujillo (42)

**Silhouette:** compact and square-shouldered, always one hand busy.

**Look:** short, thick black hair with a single grey streak, cut for convenience, not style. Strong brows, sharp dark eyes, sun lines at the corners. Big tortoiseshell sunglasses pushed up on her head. Silver earrings shaped like a pintadera, the ancient Canario clay seal.

**Costume:** a men's linen shirt in faded rust, sleeves rolled to the elbow; khaki trousers; scuffed desert boots. The Land Rover keys hang on a carabiner from her belt loop. For the mountain (Act 3): a battered olive field jacket.

**Props:** a crushed pack of Coronas, the local Canarian cigarettes, and a lighter that never works first time.

**Palette:** rust, khaki, sun-faded denim, silver.

**Signature gesture:** lowers the sunglasses to look over them when unimpressed, which is most of the time. **Idle:** lighting a cigarette on the third try; flicking ash.

**Voice:** low, smoky and quick, with a Canarian accent: soft, swallowed s's and a lilt closer to the Caribbean than to Madrid. Dry; never raises it.

### Rafa (Rafael, 23)

**Silhouette:** long and loose, always leaning on something.

**Look:** lean and sun-browned, clearly an adult in his early twenties. Dark curly hair a bit too long, pushed back; a light stubble; a wide, easy smile. Charcoal and paint on his fingers that never quite washes off.

**Costume:** an open short-sleeved shirt, white or cobalt, over a vest; cut-off jeans; old canvas sneakers. A thin silver chain. A pencil behind his ear.

**Props:** napkins he draws on, a stick of charcoal, a bar towel over his shoulder.

**Palette:** white, indigo, charcoal, one bright cobalt accent.

**Signature gesture:** sketches without looking down while he talks to you. **Idle:** drumming a rhythm on the bar; tucking the pencil back behind his ear.

**Voice:** warm, light and amused, Canarian-accented, laughing easily. Unhurried in the way only twenty-three-year-olds are.

**Design note:** he should look like Alan at twenty-three would have wanted to look: free, careless, at home in his body. Nothing about him should read as a threat; that's what makes the night dangerous.

### Dr. Rayco Baute (56)

**Silhouette:** tall, gaunt and very still.

**Look:** a long, weathered face, deep-tanned from thirty-odd years of digs. Silver hair swept back; a short, neat grey beard. Dark, deep-set eyes that hold your gaze. Reading glasses on a cord. Hands cracked and scarred from fieldwork.

**Costume:** in town, a sand-coloured linen suit (not cream, to keep it distinct from Alan's), open-collared shirt, no tie. At the dig, shirtsleeves, a faded field hat and knee pads. Always worn: his grandmother's pendant, a small carved pintadera on a leather cord.

**Props:** a brush in his breast pocket, a field notebook, the obsidian disc he hides.

**Palette:** sand, ochre and volcanic black.

**Signature gesture:** touches the pendant when he's moved, or when he lies. **Idle:** standing at a window, looking toward the sea.

**Voice:** deep, soft and precise; flawless academic English with a Canarian edge. Charming, never raised, which makes him more frightening when he's calm.

## The relationship arc

The mystery is the plot; this is the story. Every act turns on a beat between Alan and Vasco.
- **The holiday that isn't.** Alan expects a week in the sun with his boyfriend. Vasco is working, radiant and needed by other people. Alan is proud of him and jealous of the cave.
- **The secret.** Alan recognises the Sea and says nothing, because saying it would make Vasco's dream week about Alan's ghosts.
- **The break-in.** He acts alone and gets caught in front of Vasco's new world. It's the first time Alan's avoidance costs Vasco something real.
- **The fight.** Vasco says the thing he's been swallowing for eight months, and tells Alan about Lorenzo. He leaves for the mountain alone.
- **The lie.** Alan goes looking for the man he was at twenty-three, and lies to Rafa about having someone. In an open relationship the sex was never the problem: he breaks both of their rules (never to settle a fight, tell first) and lies to two people in one night.
- **The choice.** The airport or Carmen's door. It echoes his grandfather in the first game: Joaquim ran; Alan doesn't.
- **The rescue that isn't.** Vasco has already half-saved himself. Alan arrives to help, not to rescue, and the difference matters to both of them.
- **The lock.** They solve it together: Vasco knows where the light must fall; Alan chooses to stand in the water to put it there.
- **The confession.** Afterwards, before Vasco can ask, Alan tells him about Rafa. Vasco already half-knows (Yeray's "Portuguese guy" at the camp on Saturday), so what matters is that Alan says it before he's asked. Vasco doesn't forgive him on the spot. He says: "Thank you for telling me first." The ending is honest, not neat.

**The last line.** On the road down, in the Land Rover Carmen has lent them, Alan drives and Vasco puts his hand over Alan's on the gearstick. "Ask me again in Santo Domingo." It leaves the relationship alive, unsettled and pointed at the next game.

## Structure

Seven days in three acts. Each day is a chapter with its own chapter card and transition paintings, like the first game.

### Act 1: Las Palmas (Tue 18 – Thu 20 June)

**Tue 18: Arrival.** Alan lands at Gando, minus his luggage, and is sunburnt within an hour. Vasco, on site for a week already, meets him radiant and fluent in a world Alan isn't part of. Reception at El Museo Canario: Baute charming, Carmen smoking on the steps and rude to everyone. At dusk on Las Canteras beach a black mist rolls in against the heat. Alan smells the Lisbon cistern. He says nothing to Vasco, and calls Inês from a phone box. That night, the first love scene: easy, familiar and funny, everything Alan is afraid of.

**Wed 19: The island.** Vasco goes up to the dig at dawn. Alan wanders: La Isleta, where Doña Pino shows him a corroded 1478 helmet that came up in her nets; the old town of Vegueta; Santa Ana cathedral, where he finds a bell with the Sanches mark. At the museum he notices a conservation list: obsidian discs removed from Cave 6, one a day. Carmen tells him it's the panza de burro, every June, and to go to the beach, too fast, with her back to him. (She has had Elena's message on her answering machine for six months and hasn't let herself hear it. Play the brush-off as denial, not ignorance.)

**Information flow.** Alan learns something every day, from two sources. **Carmen is the source on the Canarios**, and Alan visits her archive almost daily: Wednesday she brushes him off but hands him Abreu Galindo's history; Thursday morning she explains the harimaguadas, the five-year conquest (Rejón 1478, Ansite 1483, *Atis Tirma*), Rayco's lecture-legend of the priestesses calling the sea and shutting it out at the cave, and the Artenara grandmothers' saying that the cave holds the sea down; Friday afternoon, after the break-in, she makes him coffee and explains how the cave works (the beam's path, the triangles as arrows, the discs as mirrors, "a friend of mine thought"), and why San Juan matters, then tells him, frightened, to go home before Sunday; Sunday she gives him Elena. **Inês is the source on the Sea and the founders**: Tuesday, the Sea is back; Thursday, the 1751 foundry ledger ("para estudar a fechadura") and the 1755 page ("Fizemos o que aprendemos nas Canárias. O Mar está calado."), and that the Sea knows Alan's blood; Saturday, the decoded founders' letter. **The Castilian view comes from Casa de Colón** (Thursday, on Carmen's advice): Mercedes Quintana (60s, curator, grey cardigan in any heat, Carmen's look over half-moon glasses) shows Alan the 1484 royal inquiry into the loss of three caravels and the camp off Gáldar. A sworn witness, her ancestor Juan de Quintana, describes the sea rising black against the wind on the eve of the 1483 spring equinox, women in white singing on the cliffs, and the sea coming nightly into low places and beds for three months until it went back at sunrise on Saint John's day. The bishop called it a miracle of the Baptist, which is why Las Palmas still walks into the sea at midnight on San Juan, without knowing why. Juan, pulled from the water by a Canario fisherman, drowned in his bed eleven days later. The notary's margin note: "Nadie preguntó a las mujeres" (Nobody asked the women). In 1505 the Inquisition asked for the file and searched for "the witches of '83" for a year, and found no one.

**Thu 20: The break-in.** In the morning, Carmen's archive and Casa de Colón (see Information flow). In the evening, the museum's fundraising dinner, where Vasco presents his first findings to sponsors. Convinced Montesdeoca is behind the missing discs, Alan slips into Baute's office, where the sponsor contracts are kept. He finds something else: a photograph of a bulldozed burial ground in 1966 and a map of the south coast resorts, circled. The circled resorts are Montesdeoca's, so Alan reads it as proof against the wrong man. Baute catches him, in front of the sponsors, with Vasco watching. Baute is gracious about it, which is worse.

### Act 2: The split (Fri 21 – Sat 22 June)

**Fri 21: The fight.** The solstice. In the hotel room Vasco finally says it all, and tells Alan about Lorenzo. He leaves for Artenara with the team. In the afternoon Carmen makes Alan coffee, explains how the cave works, and tells him to go home before Sunday. Alan, alone, drifts into Vegueta at night: Rafa's bar, rum, a portrait sketched on a napkin. At the end of the bar Yeray is drinking alone and talking too much about Baute's night work. Alan, busy flirting, doesn't listen. Rafa sketches them both. "Is there someone?" "No." Alan goes home with him.

**Sat 22: The morning after.** Alan wakes to Rafa's charcoal drawing of him asleep and feels sick. In Rafa's sketchbook he finds the drawing of Yeray from the bar, with the boy's words scribbled beside it: the discs go down to the lower vault at night, and only Baute has the key. The clue was in front of him while he was busy lying. Over coffee he tells Rafa the truth, and Rafa gives him the drawing anyway. He calls Inês, who tells him to tell Vasco today. He can't reach Vasco: the dig's radio is off. Meanwhile, at Risco Caído (playable as Vasco; see Gameplay), Vasco solves the light channel and understands what Baute is doing. He refuses to give the final angle. Baute drugs him and locks him in the lower vault below the caves, so he can't warn anyone until it's done. In Las Palmas the mist floods the streets of La Isleta. Alan's plane home is tomorrow.

### Act 3: The shortest night (Sun 23 – Mon 24 June)

**Sun 23: The choice.** The midpoint choice: the airport, or Carmen's door. (Choosing the airport ends the game with a short bad ending; the player is returned to the choice.) Alan goes to Carmen and admits he's an idiot. She believes him because of Elena's last message, still on her answering machine; Alan makes her play it. Then Alan puts it together himself, and remembers the letter (the Sea strikes first at whoever understands the lock), so he knows the Sea will be coming for Vasco too: Nieves has no message from Vasco, the dig radio is still dead, and Yeray's words in the sketchbook say where the discs go at night. Vasco refused Baute something, and Baute has put him where he puts things he can't let go of. The Land Rover climbs the hairpins at dusk through the black mist and into the panza de burro, blind for a few minutes, then breaks out above the cloud into a clear night full of stars. Below them the cloud glows orange where the San Juan bonfires burn along the whole coast.

**Night of 23–24: The vault.** Vasco has saved himself most of the way. Since Saturday night the Sea's water has been rising around him, wherever he stands, without spilling through the bars; it rose faster after the sixth disc came out on Sunday morning. He has spent a night and a day digging the grille's bottom anchors out of the soft tuff threshold with Lorenzo's trowel, until the blade snapped with a centimetre left. At dusk Yeray comes down with bread and water, as Baute told him to, and Vasco, with the water at his mouth, talks him round. Alan and Carmen arrive as Yeray is levering the last anchor with a pick; together they tear the grille free and Alan pulls Vasco out. The water stays behind the bars, then sinks away. The 23° 26′ and the "A." are scratched on the wall by the grille. Baute is at Cave 6, waiting for sunrise.

**Dawn, Mon 24: The lock.** The finale (see next section).

**Mon 24: Las Palmas's birthday.** On the mountain at sunrise, Alan tells Vasco about Rafa. Carmen calls the Guardia Civil on the camp radio, fuse restored; Baute turns himself in, and Carmen rides down with him, leaving Alan and Vasco her Land Rover. The road down, and the last line. That evening the city celebrates 518 years with fireworks it doesn't know it nearly didn't have. That night, the second love scene: quieter, and a choice rather than forgiveness.

## The mystery

The first game had an impossible death, a list with Alan's name on it, layered secrets and a historical crime. This one needs all four, and a twist the first game didn't have: the lock-breaker isn't killing anyone, yet people are dying, and the next one is Alan.

### The cold open: Elena Suárez

**21 December 1995.** On the night of the winter solstice, Dr. Elena Suárez, the expedition's first sacred-light specialist, drives down from Risco Caído through the sea of cloud. In the morning her car is found at the bottom of a dry barranco, 1,200 metres above the sea. The windows are closed. Her lungs are full of seawater. The police assume a flash flood and close the file. Then nothing, for six months: the Sea answers only at a turning, and the next one is June.

Vasco has her job. He doesn't know how it became free.

### The central question

**Why are people drowning in dry places?** Two reasons, and only two. First, the Sea doesn't want to be locked again, so it strikes at anyone who understands the lock: Elena, the night she read the cave right, and Vasco, in the vault, after he does; and Alan, because he is the founders' blood. In the Lisbon cistern it recognised that blood and backed away (Game 1); now, with the door loosening, it wants that blood gone first. Second, it claims what it is owed. Everyone it once had in its grip, who was pulled out by someone else, is a debt. As the lock loosens, it comes to collect them in their beds: Pepe (hauled out of the sea in 1971 by a fishing boat), Agustín (pulled into a rowing boat in 1958), nearly Doña Pino. Alan writes his own name on Doña Pino's list anyway ("Not for the same reason. But it's coming."). Doña Pino's list is the list of debts.

### The historical mystery: the 1751 letter

Why did the Lisbon founders come to Gran Canaria? Their sealed letter, in Don Fermín's keeping, answers it. They'd heard in Lisbon of a lock in the mountains that held the Sea, and came to learn how it was made, because they were about to cast a bell for the widows and were afraid of what it might call. They learned its rules, cast their first bell here, and didn't understand in time that the lock was what made it safe. They went home and cast the widows' bell the same way.

The letter also preserves what they were told about the last priestess: she closed the lock with her own hands standing in the water, and when the door shut she went down with it. That's how Alan learns what closing it costs, in his own family's handwriting. Alan gets the letter on Wednesday, but it's in faded 18th-century Portuguese; Inês's decode arrives by fax on Saturday. Until then he knows the founders were here, not what the closer pays.

### The reveals, day by day
- **Tue 18:** The mist is the Lisbon mist. (Only Alan knows.)
- **Wed 19:** Discs are vanishing; the founders were here; Pepe Santana drowns in his bed.
- **Thu 20:** Survivors are dying. Carmen: the harimaguadas, the conquest and the legend of 1483. Casa de Colón: the Castilian inquiry, three months of sea, and the San Juan "miracle". Inês: the foundry ledger, and that the Sea knows Alan's blood. The circled map points at Montesdeoca (wrong).
- **Fri 21:** Carmen explains how the cave works and tells Alan to go home before Sunday. A lifeguard drowns in his armchair. Yeray tells the truth to a man too busy to hear it.
- **Sat 22:** Baute is the lock-breaker (Vasco's chapter, and Rafa's sketchbook). Doña Pino nearly dies. Alan understands he's on the list. Inês's fax decodes the 1751 letter: the Sea strikes first at those who understand the lock, comes for every soul taken back from it, and whoever stands in the water to close it goes down with it.
- **Sun 23:** Elena's notebook and Elena's message: she worked out the lock and the cure. Alan works out where Vasco is. Alan's choice: the airport might save his life, and he goes up the mountain anyway.
- **Mon 24:** The lock is closed by the person the Sea is coming for.

### Why it's stronger than a villain plot

Baute never kills anyone with his hands. He opened a door and let the Sea do it, the way Vicente pointed the water in Lisbon. The detective question is never only "who?" but "what is the rule?", and the answer puts Alan himself in danger. That gives the airport choice real weight: leaving the island might save his life, and staying is the bravest thing he's ever done for someone else.

### Playable Vasco (Sat 22)

One chapter, about 20–30 minutes, played as Vasco at Risco Caído. It gives Vasco agency, shows the player what Alan can't know, and makes Sunday's rescue land.
- **The channel.** Vasco reads the light channel: the engraved triangles, the sun's path, the empty sockets where the discs sat. A light-and-geometry puzzle that teaches the player the system they'll use in the finale.
- **The realisation.** He works out that the empty sockets mean the lock is being opened, not studied, and that Baute's "solstice calendar" is a key.
- **The refusal.** Baute asks for the final angle. Vasco refuses. A dialogue scene where Baute is at his most persuasive and sympathetic.
- **The vault.** Drugged and locked in the lower vault, with no way to warn anyone. Vasco scratches the closing angle on the wall by the grille for whoever comes, then starts digging out the grille's bottom anchors with Lorenzo's trowel. That night salt water appears and starts to rise around him, and he understands why: he read the cave right, as Elena did. The chapter ends with him digging in the dark, and cuts to Alan.

Vasco plays differently from Alan: slower, observant, no jokes. His looks are a historian's ("Pick marks, nine hundred years old. Someone cut this in a hurry."). Where Alan talks his way through, Vasco reads his way through.

### The red herring: Montesdeoca

For the first three days, the obvious suspect is the man with the money.
- **Wed:** Alan sees Montesdeoca's company van at the museum, loading crates (actually new display cases he's donating). Chano's gossip: Montesdeoca wants to build a "Guanche-themed" hotel in Artenara and needs the site to be worthless.
- **Thu:** Carmen, who loathes him, half-believes it. Alan breaks into Baute's office to find the sponsor contracts. The circled map shows Montesdeoca's resorts, and Alan reads it as Montesdeoca's plan.
- **Sat:** Yeray's words in Rafa's sketchbook turn it around: the resorts are circled because they're Baute's targets, not Montesdeoca's projects.
- **Payoff:** Montesdeoca is vulgar and greedy, and wrong about plenty, but innocent of this. On Sunday the guests of his Las Canteras hotel, Gail and Derek among them, are exactly the people the Sea will drown: in the city, not the south.

### The night and the confession

**The night is forced, but the player feels the pull.** The player can try to leave Rafa's bar three times; each time Alan finds a reason to stay ("One more." "He's not even answering his radio." "I'm just talking."). When Rafa asks "Is there someone?", the dialogue offers two options: "No." and "…No." Both are lies. The player sees exactly where Alan's head is, and can't save him from it. Then a painting, and morning.

**The confession is a player action with real choices.** On the mountain at sunrise, the player chooses when to start, and how: the plain truth, the truth with excuses ("You'd left. I was drunk."), or the truth blaming himself too much. Vasco's reply changes with it. The plain truth earns "Thank you for telling me first." Excuses earn a long silence before it. The core ending doesn't change, but the tone of the last night does: warm and tentative after the plain truth, careful and quiet after excuses.

## The finale: Cave 6 at sunrise

**The situation.** Six sockets are empty. Without Vasco's number, Baute has turned the seventh disc to his own best reckoning of the door. Close enough: with six discs gone, the San Juan beam will fall wrong across the triangles and the lock will open completely. Seawater is already rising through the floor of a cave a thousand metres above the sea.

**The puzzle: two halves, two people.**
- **Vasco's half: where.** From the angle he scratched in the vault, the channel's geometry and the sun's position, he works out where each of the six discs must sit, and the seventh must be turned back to 23° 26′, so the beam falls true. The player helps him read the triangles and the sun line. Vasco knows where, and has to stay on the floor calling it.
- **Alan's half: placing.** The discs must be set by someone standing in the water at the door, and Alan chooses to be that someone ("It's coming for me anyway. I'd rather it found me doing something useful."). When Alan steps into the rising water, it recognises him, goes quiet, and waits for him to slip. He has to hold his nerve while the water climbs, placing each disc where Vasco tells him, in the order Vasco calls. Wrong placements make the water surge. When the lock shuts, the draining water drags Alan off the ladder and down through the door, as it took the priestess, and he stops fighting. Vasco grabs his wrist and won't let go; Carmen holds Vasco, Yeray holds Carmen, Baute holds Yeray. The priestess died because she was alone in the water; Alan lives because he isn't. Vasco: "I've let go of people before. I wasn't going to do it twice."
- **Carmen's part:** the light. She holds a mirror on the cave mouth to buy time while clouds cross the sun.
- **Baute's part:** the missing disc. From the cave mouth he sees a black stain spreading through the cloud over Las Palmas, while the south coast and its hotels lie clear and untouched in the sun. He understands. He gives Alan the sixth disc, the one he'd kept back: "It was never going to listen. Was it."

**The lock.** The beam falls true across the triangles. The water in the cave draws back into the rock with a sound like a breath. Far below, through the cave mouth, the sea flattens, and for one moment something enormous settles beneath it. Nobody comments. The sun rises over a sea of cloud. Below, the black mist sinks back and is gone, and only the ordinary grey of the panza de burro is left over the city.

**Why it works.** Neither of them could do it alone, which is the point. Vasco's knowledge without Alan's nerve fails, and so does the reverse. The puzzle is the relationship.

**Rules carried over from the first game:** never show the Sea; one glimpse only; no line gives it a voice.

**New mechanic: the phone box.** Calls to Inês replace the first game's walk-and-talk with her. She hints, researches and judges, and every call costs pesetas. Running out of coins is a gentle hint system: she's always there, but Alan has to go and get change, which means talking to people.

## Tone and themes

**Tone.** Hotter than Lisbon, grey and close down in the city under the panza de burro, blazing above it in the mountains, and funnier in Act 1: Alan as a sunburnt, jealous tourist at an academic party is pure comedy. It darkens through Act 2 into something more adult than the first game: no flings that mean nothing, only one that means too much. Horror stays where it was: felt, never shown.

**Themes.**
- **Staying is harder than choosing.** The first game was about committing. This one is about what happens on the two-hundred-and-fortieth morning.
- **Erasure and memory.** Conquest and tourism each erased part of the island. Baute wants revenge for it; Carmen wants it remembered; Vasco wants it understood. The game sides with memory.
- **You can't aim grief.** Vicente wanted the Sea to bring someone back. Baute wants it to punish someone. Both think it will do what they mean. It does what it's asked, keeps its own accounts, and takes whatever's nearest.
- **Honesty over neatness.** The ending doesn't fix Alan and Vasco. It makes them honest, which is better.

**The flirt and romance content.** One affair, with consequences, replaces the first game's three light flings. Keep the Rafa scene warm and real, so the guilt lands. The two love scenes with Vasco, on arrival night and the last night, should show eight months of intimacy: familiar, funny and easy, which is exactly what scares Alan.

## Decisions
- **Love scenes with Vasco:** two, on arrival night (Tue 18) and the last night (Mon 24).
- **The Sanches bell in Santa Ana:** required lore.
- **The airport choice:** a short bad-ending scene, then the player returns to the choice.
- **Rafa:** appears in this game only.
- **Zé, Rui and Duarte:** absent from this game.
- **Historical details** (Magec, Risco Caído, Ansite, San Juan 1996) are treated as correct.
- **Open relationship:** kept from game 2 on. The Rafa night breaks its two rules (tell first; never to settle a fight), not exclusivity.
- **Disc logic:** seven sockets. Disc 1 out on the winter solstice, discs 2–6 out Wed–Sun. The seventh stays; Baute needs its opening angle. 23° 26′ is the closing angle.

- **Elena's message is still on Carmen's answering machine.** Carmen has never erased it. On Sunday, Alan hears it in her flat. It's why she believes him.

### Vasco's chapter, part 1: the camp and Cave 6

*Saturday 22 June 1996. Playable as Vasco, about 15 minutes for this part. Music: Vasco's Theme, sparse, then Carmo-like stillness in the cave. Vasco hasn't slept since the fight.*

#### 1. The camp, 07:00

**[PLAY]** The dig camp at Risco Caído, above the cloud. Tents, a finds table under a tarpaulin, a generator. Below the cliff edge, the panza de burro lies flat and white to the horizon like a frozen sea.

*Looks:*
- **The sea of cloud:** "Alan would make a joke about this. Something about heaven, and being overdressed for it." [pause] "I'm not thinking about Alan."
- **The camp radio:** "Off." [pause] "Good. I don't want to talk to him either." (He doesn't know yet that it's been disabled.)
- **Elena Suárez's drawings,** in the expedition files on the finds table: careful sun-path charts of Cave 6 by date and hour. "Suárez. My predecessor." [pause] "Good hand. Better than mine." *(Take: the drawings go into the inventory.)*
- **His own notebook:** "Twelve pages of measurements and one sentence I've crossed out four times." (Look again: "It says 'I'm sorry'. It's not for the cave.")

**Yeray,** hungover, making coffee on a camping stove.

**YERAY** (yeray_v_01): Morning, doctor. Coffee? It's terrible.

**VASCO** (vasco_v_01): Then yes.

**YERAY** (yeray_v_02): I was in Vegueta last night. Rafa's bar. [pause] There was a Portuguese guy there. Funny. Sad, though.

**VASCO** (vasco_v_02): Most Portuguese men are. It's the national sport.

**YERAY** (yeray_v_03): [pause] Doctor. The discs. Dr. Baute says they're going down to Las Palmas, for conservation.

**VASCO** (vasco_v_03): Yes.

**YERAY** (yeray_v_04): They're not going to Las Palmas. [pause] Forget it. I'm hungover.

He walks off before Vasco can ask. If the player talks to him again: **YERAY** (yeray_v_05): "Ask Dr. Baute. Please. Not me."

*Design note: the player knows exactly who the Portuguese guy was. Vasco doesn't. Let it sting.*

#### 2. Cave 6, 09:00: reading the light

**[PLAY]** Cave 6. A domed chamber cut into soft volcanic rock, walls covered in engraved triangles, all pointing down. High in the dome a narrow channel opens to the sky; a blade of sunlight falls through it and moves slowly across the wall. Along the channel's lip are seven sockets. Five are empty. Two still hold obsidian discs, black and polished like mirrors.

*Looks:*
- **The triangles:** "Fertility symbols, the textbooks say." [pause] "They're arrows. Every one of them points down."
- **The empty sockets:** "Fresh tool marks. Steel, not stone. Someone's been prying these out with a modern chisel."
- **A remaining disc:** "Obsidian, polished to a mirror. I can see myself in it. I look like a man who hasn't slept."
- **The beam:** "My whole career has been churches built so the light lands on the altar on the right morning. The priestesses did it first. And better."
- **A white crust low on the wall:** *(look)* "Salt." [pause] "A thousand metres above the sea. That isn't possible."

**The puzzle.** Vasco has Elena's sun-path drawings, his clinometer, a compass and chalk. The player can *wait* in half-hour steps to move the beam, and *turn* the two remaining discs in their sockets.
- Mark where the beam falls at three times of day, using Elena's drawings to know where it *should* fall. It lands on bare rock: the beam is off its path.
- Turn a remaining disc. The beam bends, and lands on a triangle. The discs aren't decoration: they steer the light.
- Using Elena's charts, work out where the beam would fall with all seven discs in place on the San Juan sunrise: dead centre of the largest triangle, at the back of the cave.
- Measure the angle the last disc must hold to put it there.

**VASCO** (vasco_v_04): Twenty-three degrees, twenty-six minutes. [pause] The tilt of the world. Of course it is.

#### 4. The refusal

**[CUT, with dialogue choices]** Baute pours two cups and sits on a rock at the cave mouth, the sea of cloud behind him. He is warm, unhurried and completely sincere. The player chooses Vasco's replies; the choices change tone, not outcome.

#### 5. The vault

**[CUT]** Baute asks Vasco to come and see the discs "so you know they're safe". Vasco refuses, and sets off on foot down the road to Artenara to raise the alarm (the Land Rover keys are gone). Three hundred metres down, the coffee takes hold: Baute's thermos was laced with Elena's back sedative from the camp kit. Yeray and Baute carry him to the granary. Below the caves, a Canario granary vault cut into the rock: an iron grille, wooden crates. Vasco wakes on the steps behind the locked grille, with a headache and the taste of cinnamon.

**BAUTE** (baute_v_12): I'm sorry. [pause] I'll come for you on Monday. You have my word.

**VASCO** (vasco_v_12): Your word's under a hotel, Rayco.

Baute's footsteps fade. Silence.

**[PLAY]** Darkness, silence and a weak pocket torch with a dying battery. From Saturday night, the Sea's water: it rises around Vasco wherever he stands and does not spill through the bars.

*Looks:*
- **The silence:** "Nothing. Not a sound from the camp." [pause] "Think. You have until Monday. Think."
- **The crates:** five of them, each holding an obsidian disc wrapped in cloth. "The missing nails. All here." [pause] "Five out, two still in the cave. Tomorrow he takes the sixth."
- **The walls:** "Pick marks. Nine hundred years old." [pause] Low behind the last crate, a worn carving: a coiled serpent. He counts the coils (seven) and says nothing.
- **The grille:** "Nineteenth-century iron, new padlock. Rayco always did respect the original fabric."

**The puzzle.** The grille's top is set in a hard basalt lintel and its side is chained and padlocked, but its two bottom anchors are leaded into the soft tuff threshold, and the lead has gone powdery. With Lorenzo's trowel, Vasco digs them out by feel, with the torch off to save the battery. If both come out, the grille may swing out at the bottom on its chain, like a cat flap.

#### 6. The mark

**[PLAY, then CUT]** By the grille, Vasco takes out a small silver trowel.

*Look at the trowel:* "Lorenzo gave me this in Rome. 'For the day you find something worth keeping.'" [pause] "I thought I had."

He scratches into the soft rock: **23° 26′**. He hesitates. Then, underneath, a single letter: **A.**

His knees go wet. Salt water on the floor of a granary a thousand metres up, and rising. He understands: Elena read the cave right and it came for her that night; this morning he read it right too. He keeps digging. The torch flickers. In the dark, quietly:

**VASCO** (vasco_v_13): Pai nosso, que estais no céu... [pause]

**VASCO** (vasco_v_14): I'm sorry. I've been working on a Saturday. [pause] I know You're busy.

**VASCO** (vasco_v_15): [pause] If You can't come... send the idiot.

The torch goes out. **Cut to Alan,** on the hotel bed in Las Palmas, staring at the phone.

#### Notes
- **The five discs in the vault** are the discs Alan places in the finale. Freeing Vasco on Sunday night also recovers them. Baute keeps the sixth, and hands it over in Cave 6. The seventh never leaves its socket. Baute drugged Vasco's coffee (Elena's back sedative from the camp kit) when he tried to walk down to Artenara. Baute didn't foresee the Sea coming for him in the vault. Vasco does most of the escape himself and only needs Yeray for the last centimetre, which is what makes Yeray's choice matter.
- **23° 26′ is the tilt of the Earth's axis,** the latitude of the Tropic of Cancer, where the sun stands at the solstice. It's a real number with real meaning, and it's the number Vasco scratched for "whoever comes". It's the closing angle. The opening angle, the one Baute wanted, Vasco never writes down.
- **The "A."** is the most Vasco can say after the fight. Alan sees it on Sunday. He doesn't joke.
- **The silver trowel** is Lorenzo's. On the mountain on Monday, after the confession, Vasco gives it to Alan, half its blade snapped off in the vault: "For the day you find something worth keeping. [pause] Keep it."

## Series layer

Additions from The Sea — Mythology of the Alan Sanches Series. This bible wins wherever the two differ; these only add.
- **Law 9: it never forgets what was taken back from it.** Anyone the Sea had in its grip and lost to someone else's hands stays owed, and when a lock loosens it comes to collect them. Separately, when a lock loosens, it strikes first at anyone who understands how to shut it.
- **The lock is an uninvitation, not a chain.** The Harimaguadas didn't fight the Sea; Magec shuts it out again with every sunrise. That keeps the game consistent with the series rule that the Sea can't be fought, only uninvited. Vasco's "nails" are his reading, and half wrong: the discs don't hold the Sea down, they keep the door shut.
- **The 1751 bell's godparents (proposal).** The Quintanas stood godparents to the Las Palmas bell. Descended from a Harimaguada through a conquest-era marriage, they knew what the Sea did in 1483 and would never call it; they took the role so the bell's voice belonged to a family that would keep it silent. Keepers, not callers. Mercedes Quintana at Casa de Colón is that descendant. In this game she only guards the 1484 inquiry and warns Alan off the low places; the godparent role and the Harimaguada descent stay unspoken.
- **Ambrose Pell, unseen.** One deniable trace only: a salvage company's name on a boat in the harbour, or a letter to Baute on headed paper. He's the shadow behind every game until St. Augustine, and he's married to Alan's mother.
- **Pedro's trace.** One faint, never-confirmed sign of Alan's drowned father: a whistle in the panza, a wet footprint by the hotel bed. Never explained.
- **The serpent mark.** Vasco finds it, low and old, in Cave 6 or the vault, in his playable chapter. He counts the coils and says nothing about it.
- **San Borondón as background colour.** Someone claims to have seen the phantom island on San Juan. It's the Sea showing what it keeps; nobody in the game says so.
- **The Sea is never funny.** All comedy stays in the human layer.
- **Where the Brotherhood learned bell magic.** The 1751 founders studied the Harimaguadas' lock and learned its rules there: the Sea hears only what is given to it in water, answers only at a turning, and must be shut out again at every turning. Their own addition was the bell, a voice that carries farther than any rite. Las Palmas was their first bell, but it hangs behind the Harimaguadas' lock, so while the lock holds it has no power at all. Lisbon's was made the same way, where nothing shut the Sea out. If Baute opens the lock, the Las Palmas bell wakes: that's what Pell, unseen, is really waiting for. The discovery in Santa Ana should let the player feel it: the Brotherhood's secret was never theirs.
